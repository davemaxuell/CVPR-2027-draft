Abstract
The cross-entropy method (CEM) uses world-model scores to select action
sequences and fit the distribution sampled in its next iteration. A scoring
error can therefore change both the present decision and the candidates
considered later. We evaluate these two roles separately. Four types of
predictive model generate CEM traces, and every model rescores every saved
candidate pool. Executing the same candidates in the environment provides a reference
elite set and proposal update.
Across twelve independently trained task-seed units on Walker and Cheetah,
the pre-specified proposal distance falls from the first to the final
CEM iteration in every unit.
Proposal widths contract and fitted means separate relative to the remaining
search width. Pairwise ranking agreement stays near chance on Walker and
declines on Cheetah; elite-set agreement does not improve. This comparison shows greater
variation between scorers than between pool sources on Cheetah; Walker has
variation in both and in their pairings. We use the original six units to
select Random nonlinear for a one-update intervention, without inspecting
intervention outcomes. Replacing its first model-ranked update with an
environment-ranked update lowers final realised selected-sequence cost in
those six units and in six further units held out from the selection.
1 Introduction
A world model used for planning determines which actions receive further
consideration. In model-predictive control (MPC), the planner predicts the
outcomes of candidate action sequences, selects a sequence, and executes its
first action before replanning. The cross-entropy method (CEM) performs this
search by repeatedly sampling candidates and fitting a new sampling
distribution to those with the lowest predicted
cost [Bharadhwaj et al., 2020]. We call that distribution the proposal
and the retained candidates the elite set.
As an illustrative example, consider two candidate pools for the same physical state and cost objective.
Pool A contains a sequence with realised cost 1, but the scorer selects one
with realised cost 5. This is a selection error: a lower-cost sequence was
available. Pool B contains only sequences with realised cost 8 or higher. Its scorer orders
every candidate correctly and selects the cost-8 sequence. Earlier CEM
updates could have left the planner searching this second region. Correct
ordering within that pool still produces a higher-cost choice than the
misranked choice from pool A.
A fixed-pool comparison with environment executions can measure ranking
errors, elite membership, proposal-refit disagreement, and selection regret
among the available candidates. In the example, it can expose the error in
pool A and confirm the ordering in pool B. It cannot show which lower-cost
sequences another proposal would have sampled. Correct within-pool ordering
does not ensure that the pool contains low-cost actions.
Evaluation also depends on where the candidates came from. Logged rollouts
use a data-collection policy; actor-centred ranking tests perturb a reference
action sequence. These tests can measure prediction or ordering on their
chosen action distributions. CEM changes its own distribution after each
elite decision. We call this planner-induced query shift: model
scores change the optimiser’s future queries. The physical system need not
change.
Rank agreement measured along a CEM trace already captures how ordering
changes during search [Wang et al., 2026]. Our comparison adds
pools produced by other models’ update histories. Applying the same scorer
to those pools tests whether its disagreement depends on pool source;
applying different scorers to one pool compares their decisions on identical
actions. Realised candidate costs separately record which pools contain
lower-cost sequences.
Our comparison uses four predictors fitted over frozen Dreamer
contexts [Hafner et al., 2020]. A context is the backbone’s internal
representation of the observation history. Each predictor replaces the full
Dreamer dynamics during planning, so we call it a surrogate. It
predicts future contexts under a candidate action sequence. A fixed readout
maps those contexts to the task variables used in the planning cost. The
four surrogates share data, transition form, cost, and CEM settings; their
feature maps differ.
Each surrogate first runs its own CEM search. We save the candidate pool at
every iteration, execute its candidates from the same restored simulator
state, and then score that pool with every surrogate. This separates two
roles: the generator produced the pool through its earlier updates;
the scorer ranks the saved pool now. We call this a crossed design
because each of the four generators is paired with each of the four scorers,
giving all 16 combinations. A column follows one
scorer across pool sources. A row holds the pool fixed and compares scorers.
The matrix describes proposal disagreement associated with generators,
scorers, and their pairings. It does not assign causal effects to those
components.
The intervention tests a consequence that rescoring alone cannot establish.
At the first update, we fit one proposal from model-ranked elites and another
from environment-ranked elites in the same pool. Both branches then continue
with the original scorer and matched random samples. This one-update
splice changes how later candidates are generated through one earlier
decision. Its endpoint is the realised cost of the sequence selected after
the remaining CEM iterations. Figure 1 shows the audit
and intervention together.

2 Related work
Model evaluation has long depended on the decisions that use the model.
Value-aware model learning weights errors through downstream value
functions [Farahmand et al., 2017]. DeepMDP connects reward and latent
transition prediction to the properties of the learned
representation [Gelada et al., 2019]. Value equivalence compares models
through their effects on specified value functions and
policies [Grimm et al., 2020]. Lambert et al. [2020] examine the mismatch between
one-step prediction likelihood and downstream control performance. These
works give distinct reasons to evaluate a model through its intended use.
We hold the predictors fixed and examine the finite candidate decisions
made by CEM.
Yu et al. [2026] make a broader case for evaluating world models by
interventional action fidelity, policy ranking, and optimisation utility.
Their proposed reset-matched action branches are directly relevant to our
environment reference: every candidate starts from the same physical state.
Bhamidipaty et al. [2026] define model exploitation through a reversal
of policy preference between the model and the environment, and analyse when
such reversals can occur. Our elite and ranking measurements examine finite
candidate disagreements; they do not estimate the worst-case exploitability
of a model over a policy class.
The closest CEM diagnostic is the CEM-stage Spearman analysis of
Wang et al. [2026]. It measures agreement between model and realised
cost rankings as search moves from initial candidates towards concentrated
and elite populations. This already evaluates the model on queries made
during search. Our crossed design compares a scorer’s own pools with pools
generated by other surrogates. Holding the scorer fixed tests how its
disagreement varies with pool source. Holding the pool fixed compares
scorers without changing candidates. We also compare the means and widths
fitted from the two elite sets, because these parameters determine the next
queries. A rank correlation alone does not specify that refit.
Li et al. [2026] execute all candidates in saved proposal pools to
distinguish failed selection from the absence of a feasible candidate.
Their adjacent-set method then reconstructs an action sequence from a
neighbourhood of low-cost candidates. We use the same basic control of
executing candidates from a common initial state. Our intervention changes
an earlier elite refit and follows its consequence through later pools. This
tests how the proposal history changes the candidates available at final
selection.
Several recent methods change components that our audit keeps fixed.
Chahe et al. [2026] train a cost-ranking objective that penalises
increasing action perturbations. Huang et al. [2026] add a
trajectory-level cost to endpoint scoring, and Singh [2026]
change the planning objective without retraining the predictor.
Sommer and Schilling [2026] learn a plan-improvement operator through
imagined rollouts. These methods change candidate scores or proposal updates.
Applying the crossed comparison before and after such a change would test
which measured disagreements it reduces.
3 Auditing a CEM update
The audit begins with a fixed planning problem: one saved simulator state and
its matching Dreamer context. We run CEM from that context, save each sampled
pool, and compare the decisions obtained from model predictions with those
obtained from executing the same candidates. Restoring the simulator state
before every execution prevents one candidate from changing the starting
point of another. Realised cost supplies the reference ordering within that
pool.
Candidates and elites.

Pairwise order agreement covers the rest of the pool. It counts the fraction
of candidate pairs for which the scorer preserves the environment ordering,
excluding pairs tied in realised cost. Selected-candidate regret instead
subtracts the best realised cost in the pool from the realised cost of the
scorer’s top candidate. It measures the cost of selection within the
available pool. Neither measurement says what candidates the next proposal
will produce. We retain Spearman agreement, Jaccard elite overlap, and
alternative elite cuts as supporting records.
3.1 Comparing the two proposal refits
The elite indices alone do not determine the size of an update error. Two
disjoint elite sets could contain very similar actions and fit similar
proposals. Conversely, a small change in elite membership could move the
proposal if the changed sequences lie far apart. We therefore apply CEM’s
actual refit to both elite sets.
For each coordinate, CEM fits the elite mean and standard deviation, flooring
the standard deviation at 0.05. The model elites produce

The first action.
MPC executes only the first of CEM’s 15 planned actions before replanning.
A distance averaged over the full plan can conceal disagreement in that
action. We therefore also compute DDD using only its six coordinates.
We also compare the first actions of the scorer’s lowest-cost sequence and
the environment’s lowest-cost sequence. Their root-mean-square gap is
reported in raw action units and after division by the current first-action
proposal widths. The raw gap records separation on the bounded action
scale. The normalised gap records separation relative to the region CEM
is still searching. A shrinking proposal can make these quantities move
in opposite directions.
Clipping and the standard-deviation floor provide two further checks on that
interpretation. We record proposal width, floor occupancy, the clipping
rate, and how far clipping displaces the sampled pool mean. These records
distinguish contraction of the proposal from changes introduced by its
bounds.
4 Crossed audit and one-update splice
Crossed audit.
Every surrogate receives the same actor-centred proposal and random samples at
iteration zero. Later pools inherit the elite decisions made along each trace.
The generator ggg produced a pool through those preceding decisions; the
scorer fff ranks that fixed, realised pool.
All generator traces use the same standard-normal samples at every iteration.
Their bounded candidates match at iteration zero because their proposal
parameters also match. Later candidates differ when generators fit different
means or standard deviations. Common samples remove independent Monte Carlo
variation from the row comparison.

The matrix supports comparisons that a diagonal trace cannot provide.

generators. That appears as a high-distance column and associates the
disagreement with current scoring. Suppose instead that all four scorers
produce larger distances on one generator’s pool. That appears as a
high-distance row and associates the disagreement with the queried actions.
The physical state, task cost, and candidate count remain fixed in both
comparisons.

We summarise row, column, and residual variation by their shares of the
matrix’s sum of squares. Each share is calculated within one task-seed
matrix, then averaged over the six seeds for that task. Decomposing an
already averaged matrix would discard between-seed differences in its
structure. These percentages describe the four fixed families; they are
not population variance estimates.
Appendix B gives the exact calculation.
Row differences alone do not show that an earlier elite decision caused
harm. Rescoring measures disagreement on the inherited pool; an
intervention is needed to test an update’s consequence.
The distance matrix also does not measure the lowest cost available in a
pool. Even perfect agreement with the environment elites can occur when
every candidate has high realised cost. We retain realised candidate costs
to distinguish that coverage problem from disagreement within the pool.
At iteration zero, the four generator labels must point to identical
candidate IDs and environment costs. We verify this before analysing later
pools. Row differences can appear only after the first model-ranked update.
One-update splice.
To test an elite decision’s consequence, we fork the search at iteration
zero. Every generator has the same proposal and candidates at this point.
Using this common starting pool allows the two branches to differ in one
elite set without inheriting an earlier difference in search history.
Branch MMM fits its next proposal from model elites; branch EEE fits from
environment elites. Both use the same mean and standard-deviation refit,
including the width floor.
From iteration one, the original surrogate scores both branches. Matched
standard-normal samples are scaled and shifted by the two inherited proposal
states. All subsequent elite sets are selected using the original surrogate.
We reuse the model branch from the audit and execute the new candidates in
branch EEE.
After each iteration, the controller retains the lowest predicted-cost
sequence seen so far, including candidates from earlier pools. This
incumbent is the sequence that supplies its first action if planning
stops at that point. We evaluate each branch’s incumbent after iterations
one, two, and three. The primary endpoint is the branch-EEE realised cost
minus the branch-MMM realised cost after iteration three, the final CEM
iteration. A negative contrast favours the environment-elite branch.
The intermediate endpoints show whether the contrast changes direction
during the remaining search; they cannot replace the fixed final endpoint.
We also record the lowest realised candidate cost reached by each branch.
A branch can contain a lower-cost candidate without selecting it, so we
compare the best available cost with the incumbent’s realised cost.
Only the first update is replaced; later-stage interventions are not tested.
Environment elites require realised costs for every candidate, so the splice
is an oracle diagnostic rather than a deployable planner. Its endpoint
measures a selected sequence’s finite-horizon cost, not closed-loop episode
return.
5 Experimental setting
The crossed audit contains 12 task-seed units: six Walker seeds and six
Cheetah seeds. Each unit has an independently trained Dreamer backbone and
actor [Hafner et al., 2020]. We freeze both. The backbone maps observation
history to a 1280-dimensional context c_{t}ctc_{t}; the actor supplies the initial CEM
mean. Four separately fitted surrogates propagate c_{t}ctc_{t} under proposed actions.
Each surrogate maps the current context into a feature space, propagates those
features for 15 action-conditioned linear transitions, and decodes every
predicted state back to Dreamer’s context space. The rollout receives no
intermediate Dreamer states. Training minimises free-running decoded-context
error at horizons 1, 3, 5, 8, and 15, plus reward and continuation losses. A
separate validation split selects the checkpoint.
The four families change the feature map \varphiφ\varphi and its decoder.
Identity uses
\varphi(c)=cφ⁡(c)=c\varphi(c)=c. Learned nonlinear uses a trainable multilayer
perceptron. Anchored nonlinear concatenates trainable affine and
nonlinear features. Random nonlinear uses fixed random nonlinear
features. The three compressed families have width 256 and share the decoder
architecture, auxiliary heads, optimiser, data, and training budget. Identity
retains the 1280-dimensional context and is not width matched. All
four use the same action-conditioned transition form.
We use walker_walk and cheetah_run from the DeepMind
Control Suite [Tassa et al., 2018, Tunyasuvunakool et al., 2020, Todorov et al., 2012].
Seeds 0–2 form the original six-unit cohort used to decide which splice to
run. The follow-up rule required a pattern in at least two of three seeds
on each task, retained after omitting any one saved state. This limited
dependence on a single seed or decision point. The original questions and
rule were fixed before inspecting their outcomes. Later analyses of distance
components, raw action gaps and clipping explain the results without
changing the selected pairing. The rule governed follow-up computation;
it was not a significance test.
Appendix A gives its exact criteria and chronology.
Seeds 3–5 were fixed before inspecting their audit outcomes to increase
the precision of the same comparisons. They use the same state-selection,
replay, model and CEM settings and cannot change the original selection.
We also fixed the selected splice and its decision rule before inspecting
these six units’ splice costs. This held-out test checks whether the same
intervention lowers final selected-sequence cost on new units. It does not
compare other families as intervention targets. We report the two cohorts
separately as well as the combined task means.
Candidate scoring begins at a saved context and predicts 15 steps. A fixed
ridge readout maps decoded contexts to the task variables in the cost. Walker
tracks a fixed goal; Cheetah tracks the frozen actor’s reference trajectory.
Predicted and realised costs use the same task variables, reference targets
and cost weights. Each CEM
run has four iterations, 256 candidates, and 32 elites. The proposal width
starts at 0.35 and has a floor of 0.05. Surrogate parameters stay frozen and
training-time stochasticity is disabled.
Appendix Table 9 lists the remaining model,
training, readout, and task-cost settings.
For each unit, we export 64 simulator-state/context pairs from the frozen actor.
The audit uses one state from each of four pre-selected episode bins after a
replay check. The bins spread decision points across an episode without
selecting states for their audit outcome. We restore the exact state before
every candidate execution and verify repeated costs. Each generator trace
uses the same standard-normal samples at every iteration; differences in
its proposal parameters produce differences in its later bounded actions.
The complete crossed audit executes 196,608 candidates; the original six-unit
cohort accounts for 98,304. Each pool is executed once and then scored by all four
surrogates, so the off-diagonal cells add model evaluations without adding
simulator rollouts. We average states within each task-seed unit before
comparing seeds. The diagonal trajectories also average the four model
families; crossed matrices retain every generator-scorer cell. Candidate
sequences, matrix cells, and saved states are repeated measurements within
a unit, not independent replicates.

7 Discussion and limitations
A proposal audit needs an explicit reference scale. Mean separation in raw
action units answers a different question from separation relative to the
remaining search width. Width ratios answer another. Reporting those
components alongside elite recovery prevents contraction alone from being
read as improved decision agreement. Realised selected-sequence cost is
still needed to test whether an update changes the outcome of search.
The crossed comparison guides the next controlled test. On Cheetah, a scoring
change can be tested across several fixed pool sources, because the observed
differences follow scorer columns in every seed. On Walker, a score-only
account omits row and pairing variation. A proposal constraint could be
tested with the scorer held fixed, or a scoring change could be tested on
the same saved pools. The matrix makes these comparisons available without
additional off-diagonal simulator executions. It does not choose a remedy
or guarantee that one will work.
The Random nonlinear splice shows that one elite decision can affect the
selected cost after three further CEM iterations. Its direction
repeats on units held out from pairing selection. Other surrogates might
also benefit from an environment-elite update: we have not compared splices
across all four families. The result therefore establishes the consequence
of the selected update, without establishing that the residual identified
the best intervention target.
The experiment covers two tasks, six independently trained Dreamer
backbones per task, four states per backbone, one horizon, and one CEM
configuration. The shared transition form controls part of the model
comparison; other dynamics classes may produce different matrices.
Identity also retains a wider context than the three compressed families,
so family differences cannot be assigned to feature type alone. The four
states sample a restricted part of each actor’s experience, and state
omissions check dependence on those points rather than coverage of all
reachable states.
The audit requires a resettable environment and execution of every
candidate. It is suited to diagnosing a model-planner loop in simulation.
The splice uses information unavailable to an ordinary deployed planner,
and lower finite-horizon selected-sequence cost does not establish higher
closed-loop return. A method motivated by the audit must be evaluated
without that oracle and under receding-horizon control.
Conclusion.
A world model used by CEM determines both the ordering of current candidates
and the distribution of later queries. Crossed rescoring describes these
roles separately; a matched one-update splice tests whether an elite
decision changes the final selected-sequence cost.

Appendix A Follow-up rule and experiment chronology
The original audit used Walker and Cheetah seeds 0–2, with four saved
states, four surrogate families, and four CEM iterations per task-seed
unit. Its gate was a rule fixed before outcome inspection for deciding
whether to extend the audit and run an intervention. It was not a significance test.
Original questions.
The first question tested whether the family-mean diagonal distance DDD
increased from iteration zero to three. The second tested whether pool
source or an own-pool pairing produced a replicated pattern at iteration
three. A pool-source pattern required at least three of four scorer columns
to name the same highest-distance generator within a task; that generator
could differ between tasks. A pairing pattern required the same surrogate
to have a positive diagonal residual I_{ff3}If​f​3I_{ff3} on both tasks.
A qualifying pattern needed the stated direction on Walker and Cheetah,
at least two of three seeds per task, and the same outcome after each of
the four one-state omissions. The omissions recomputed task-seed summaries
from the remaining three states. This rule limited reliance on one seed or
one saved state. It did not estimate the probability of replication on a
new task or provide a multiple-testing correction. Only the second
question could trigger the one-update splice.
The original measurements included DDD, pairwise order agreement, and
elite recovery. Selected-candidate regret, alternative elite cuts, and
downstream family ordering did not enter the gate. We specified the
change-from-baseline matrices, descriptive sums of squares, random-elite
reference and D_{W}DWD_{W} after inspecting the Walker results, before inspecting
the completed Cheetah summaries. The mean/width decomposition, raw-action
gap, floor occupancy and clipping displacement followed inspection of the
six-unit pilot and preceded completion of the extension cohort. These
analyses could not change the original rule or the selected family.

Appendix H Random-elite reference
The magnitude of DDD depends on the proposal and the finite candidate pool. As
a reference, we draw 4,096 random subsets of 32 candidates from each saved
pool in the original seeds 0–2, sampling without replacement within each
subset. Fitting each subset produces a pool-specific
distribution of proposal distances from the environment-elite update.
Figure 9: Observed diagonal proposal distances against 32-candidate random

finite pool and does not measure realised cost.

Training the twelve backbones, fitting the 48 surrogates and running the
reported audits and splices requires approximately 179 GPU-hours in total.
The breakdown is 133 hours for the twelve 500k-step Dreamer backbones,
4.6 hours for data exports and surrogate fits, and 41 hours for the crossed
audit and twelve splice units. The first two estimates allocate 12/4812/4812/48 of
the corresponding 48-unit training budgets to the units used here.

18,432 candidate executions and 2.6 of the 41 GPU-hours.
These figures sum wall-clock device time on H200, H100, A100, L40S and
V100 GPUs without adjusting for their different speeds. They estimate the
cost of reproducing the reported experiment and exclude unused tasks,
other evaluations, failed jobs and exploratory runs.
