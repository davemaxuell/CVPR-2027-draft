Abstract
Autonomous driving requires choosing a safe and efficient plan as surrounding
traffic evolves. Generate-and-select planners propose multiple trajectories and
score them for execution, and they have outperformed representative
direct-prediction baselines on NAVSIM. Their scorer must compare plans that
were never executed. Driving logs record the future of only the executed
trajectory, so matching the logged future can leave predictions for the
alternatives unconstrained; a simulator, in contrast, can label the outcome of
every candidate. We introduce World4Scorer, which builds the scorer as a
trajectory-conditioned JEPA-style predictor: it predicts a state for each
candidate and reads the candidate’s scores from that state. Simulator outcome
labels supervise the states of all candidates, and the observed future of the
executed trajectory anchors the predictor to real scene evolution. Because one
predictor produces every candidate’s state, the anchor can constrain shared parameters used to score unexecuted plans, while the future itself is needed only
during training. Generated candidates mostly score well, so a scene-matched
bank adds low-scoring plans to the outcome supervision; framewise choices can conflict, so inertial re-ranking keeps consecutive selections consistent.
World4Scorer achieves state-of-the-art NAVSIM-v2 performance and a strong
adapted-system result on closed-loop Bench2Drive. With the LeWM world model and
planning budget fixed, outcome-based scoring also improves manipulation
planning on the OGBench-Cube benchmark.

1 Introduction
Autonomous driving requires turning sensor observations into safe,
efficient, and comfortable motion. Direct trajectory-prediction methods
such as TransFuser (Chitta et al., 2023) and UniAD (Hu et al., 2023) map scene
features to a driving plan.
Recent generate-and-select planners have achieved stronger performance than
representative direct-prediction baselines on
NAVSIM (Liao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025d; Kirby et al., 2026). They use a trajectory generator
to propose alternatives and a learned scorer to choose one for execution
(Figure 1a). To benefit from these alternatives,
the scorer must identify which plan best balances safety, progress, and comfort.
Scoring a plan requires assessing its consequences as surrounding traffic
evolves. World models provide predictive features for planning (Li et al., 2025a; Hong et al., 2026)
and candidate evaluation (Li et al., 2025b) (Figure 1b).
Their training faces a supervision gap: each driving log records the future
of the executed trajectory, while alternative trajectories have no corresponding
future observations. Matching the logged future can leave predictions for
unobserved alternatives unconstrained, even though these predictions decide which plan is selected.
Candidate outcomes fill this gap. Simulation can label whether each
alternative plan collides, leaves the drivable area, or makes progress,
without an observed visual future for that plan. When a predictor produces
the state from which each candidate is scored, these labels supervise the
state of every candidate, including plans that were never executed.
The observed future serves as an anchor: it supervises the state of the
executed trajectory and ties the shared predictor to real scene evolution.
We introduce World4Scorer, an outcome-grounded world modeling
framework in which a trajectory-conditioned predictor is the scorer
(Figure 1c). Following the joint-embedding predictive
architecture (JEPA) (Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024), the predictor maps each candidate to
a state; score heads read candidate outcomes from this state, and a visual
readout predicts the future features of the executed trajectory. The core is
this shared predictor, trained by both candidate outcomes and the observed
future. As support, a perturbed candidate bank widens outcome coverage, and
training-free inertial re-ranking checks continuity with the previous plan.
•
Outcome-grounded world modeling. We build the candidate scorer
as a trajectory-conditioned predictor. Simulator outcomes supervise the
predicted state of every generated and bank candidate, including plans that
were never executed, while the observed future of the executed plan anchors
the shared predictor to real scene evolution.
•
Complementary supervision. Outcome labels reach every candidate;
the observed future reaches one trajectory yet constrains the shared predictor.
In controlled tests, an observed-future target improves planning but
a current-frame target does not, mismatched outcome labels degrade ranking,
and reassigning candidate states lowers PDMS in every trial.
•
Evidence across benchmarks and domains. World4Scorer achieves
state-of-the-art performance on NAVSIM-v2 and leads the compared methods on
NAVSIM-v1. Cross-setting tests extend these results: an adapted system is
strong in closed-loop Bench2Drive, and outcome-based scoring improves
manipulation with a frozen world model.
2 Related Work
End-to-end autonomous driving.
TransFuser (Chitta et al., 2023) and UniAD (Hu et al., 2023) regress a trajectory
from scene features. Candidate-based planners compare alternatives
from vocabularies or generators.
VADv2 (Jiang et al., 2026) predicts probabilities over a fixed
vocabulary. DiffusionDrive (Liao et al., 2025) refines
anchors and selects by learned confidence. GTRS (Li et al., 2025d) scores
vocabulary and generated candidates, and
DriveSuprim (Yao et al., 2026a) filters candidates from coarse to fine.
CLOVER (Ang et al., 2026) trains its generator on evaluator-filtered
pseudo-experts and its scorer on evaluator sub-scores. DrivoR (Kirby et al., 2026) learns proposals and
simulator-supervised scores with separate decoders.
We follow its generator–scorer framework and make the scorer a predictor
trained by simulator labels, anchored by observed futures.
Predictive representations for planning.
Future prediction supports both trajectory generation (Li et al., 2025a; Xia et al., 2026; Hong et al., 2026)
and candidate evaluation (Li et al., 2025b; Kim et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2025). Drive-JEPA (Wang et al., 2026) uses video-JEPA pretraining to provide predictive
features for planning. We focus on the supervision available during candidate
comparison: observed futures cover executed trajectories, whereas simulator
outcomes cover every candidate; World4Scorer trains one predictor with both.
Figure 2: Overview of World4Scorer.
(a) The trajectory generator proposes 64 candidates, scored by World4Scorer
before inertial re-ranking.
(b) The visual readout and score heads train the shared predictor: future observations supervise
visual prediction for the executed trajectory; outcome labels supervise
scoring for all generated candidates and 16 bank trajectories.
3 World4Scorer
World4Scorer follows a generator–scorer framework: it proposes trajectories,
predicts a state for each candidate, and scores these states before inertial
re-ranking (Figure 2). We use “world model” in a decision-focused sense: latent states
carry candidate outcomes, not full futures.
3.1 Overview
At inference (Figure 2a), DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) with LoRA (Hu et al., 2022)
encodes four camera views into scene tokens sts_{t}. A transformer generates
N=64N=64 trajectories, 𝒞t={τt,i}i=1N\mathcal{C}_{t}=\{\tau_{t,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}.
The predictor maps each trajectory query to a state using the scene tokens.
Score heads read each state together with the ego state. Inertial re-ranking
then adds compatibility with the previous plan.
At training time (Figure 2b), winner-take-all imitation supervises
the generator. Outcome labels supervise all 64 generated candidates and 16
trajectories sampled from a scene-matched bank. A separate query for the logged
trajectory predicts its observed visual future. These three query sets use the
same predictor, allowing visual prediction and outcome scoring to train shared parameters.
3.2 Shared Prediction and Scoring
An action encoder ϕ\phi maps each candidate trajectory to a query token.
The shared predictor PP (Latent World Model in Figure 2)
processes all candidate queries together with the scene tokens:
qt,i=ϕ⁡(sg⁡[τt,i]),Qt=[qt,1,…,qt,N],Zt=P⁡(Qt,st).q_{t,i}=\phi(\operatorname{sg}[\tau_{t,i}]),\quad Q_{t}=[q_{t,1},\ldots,q_{t,N}],\quad Z_{t}=P(Q_{t},s_{t}).
(1)
Its four transformer blocks exchange information among candidates through
self-attention and incorporate scene information through cross-attention,
followed by output normalization. Row zt,iz_{t,i} of ZtZ_{t} is the predicted
state for candidate ii, and sg\operatorname{sg} stops gradients.
The score heads HH read each candidate state after adding the
encoded ego state ete_{t}: ℓt,i=H⁡(zt,i+et)\boldsymbol{\ell}_{t,i}=H(z_{t,i}+e_{t}).
They predict collision avoidance (NC), drivable-area compliance (DAC),
driving direction (DDC), time-to-collision (TTC), progress (EP), and comfort (C).
Let pt,i,m=σ⁡(ℓt,i,m)p_{t,i,m}=\sigma(\ell_{t,i,m}),
𝒢={NC,DAC,DDC}\mathcal{G}=\{\mathrm{NC,DAC,DDC}\}, and
𝒜={TTC,EP,C}\mathcal{A}=\{\mathrm{TTC,EP,C}\}. The ranking score is
J^t,i=∑m∈𝒢αm​log⁡pt,i,m+log⁡(∑m∈𝒜βm​pt,i,m).\hat{J}_{t,i}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{G}}\alpha_{m}\log p_{t,i,m}+\log\!\left(\sum_{m\in\mathcal{A}}\beta_{m}p_{t,i,m}\right).
(2)
Here αm\alpha_{m} and βm\beta_{m} are per-benchmark weights (Appendix A.2). The highest-scoring
candidate defines the framewise choice; inertial re-ranking further incorporates
previous-plan compatibility.
Detaching candidate coordinates prevents scoring gradients from reaching
the generator through its trajectory outputs. The scoring loss still trains
ϕ\phi, PP, HH, and the scene features.
3.3 Outcome-Grounded Predictive Learning
Only the executed trajectory has an observed future; generated and bank
candidates lack future observations but have outcome labels. Outcome labels
supervise every candidate state, and the observed future anchors the predictor
through the executed trajectory (Figure 2b).
Trajectory-conditioned JEPA.
Following JEPA (Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024), the predictor takes scene tokens and the
logged trajectory query. Its target is the frozen DINOv2 embedding of the
observed front view two seconds after the current frame, mean-pooled to
384 dimensions:
ztexec=P⁡([ϕ⁡(τtexec)],sg⁡[st]),utfut=fDINO​(ot+2​s).z_{t}^{\rm exec}=P([\phi(\tau_{t}^{\rm exec})],\operatorname{sg}[s_{t}]),\qquad u_{t}^{\rm fut}=f_{\rm DINO}(o_{t+2\mathrm{s}}).
(3)
The linear visual readout gg predicts this feature with a cosine loss:
ℒfut=1M​∑n=1M[1−simcos⁡(g⁡(znexec),unfut)].\mathcal{L}_{\rm fut}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{n=1}^{M}\left[1-\operatorname{sim}_{\cos}\bigl(g(z_{n}^{\rm exec}),u_{n}^{\rm fut}\bigr)\right].
(4)
Here nn indexes MM scenes. With scene tokens detached, this loss updates
ϕ\phi, PP, and gg; at deployment, the predictor needs only current observations and candidate trajectories.
Because every query passes through the same PP, this single target also
constrains the parameters that produce each candidate’s state. The self-attention
query and key projections receive no gradient from this single-query loss and
are trained by the outcome losses alone (Appendix A.2).
Learning from candidate outcomes.
Outcome labels yiy_{i} supervise H⁡(zi+e)H(z_{i}+e):
ℒout=ℒscore+λbank​ℒbank=∑iwi​ℓout​(H⁡(zi+e),yi),\mathcal{L}_{\rm out}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm score}+\lambda_{\rm bank}\mathcal{L}_{\rm bank}=\sum_{i}w_{i}\,\ell_{\rm out}\bigl(H(z_{i}+e),y_{i}\bigr),
(5)
where ii covers generated and bank candidates, ℓout\ell_{\rm out} uses each set’s
loss components, and wiw_{i} includes averaging and bank weighting
(Section 3.4). Outcome supervision thus reaches every
candidate state, including those without visual targets, and requires
distinctions in safety, progress, and comfort. A scene supplies outcome labels for up to 80 candidates and a future target for one.
Why future supervision still matters.
Models that fit the available outcome labels may still disagree on other
candidates (Grimm et al., 2020), including which one to
select (Mandi et al., 2022). We bound this variation with a local generalized Gauss–Newton model
around a fitted θ0\theta_{0}, keeping each predictor call in its own context.

Figure 3 shows how future constraints can remove
local disagreement on candidate choice. A smaller variation range makes a
candidate’s lead harder to reverse within the allowed neighborhood. The future
loss does not constrain every unexecuted candidate uniformly: it narrows a
comparison only along directions that shared parameters couple to the executed
query’s readout. Appendix C.4 proves this local result;
Section 4.3 evaluates the learning effects predicted by this mechanism.

3.4 Outcome Supervision across Candidates
Imitation training yields mostly expert-like, high-scoring candidates
(light bars in Figure 4), so adverse outcomes such as collisions
and drivable-area violations form a small share of the training examples.
Following CLOVER (Ang et al., 2026), we use a scene-matched bank of
perturbed trajectories with precomputed simulator labels. The bank covers a
broader score range, including unsafe plans (dark bars).
We sample 1616{} bank trajectories per step to complement
the generated candidates.
Figure 4: Candidate bank.
The bank adds low-scoring plans.
Both sets use separate calls to the same ϕ\phi, PP, and HH.
For generated candidates, ℒscore\mathcal{L}_{\rm score} sums binary cross-entropy
over six components, using soft targets for progress.
For bank candidates, ℒbank\mathcal{L}_{\rm bank} averages binary cross-entropy
over five non-progress components and adds 0.10.1 times the progress L1 loss.
Each component loss is averaged over scenes and candidates in its set.
Winner-take-all L1 imitation, ℒWTA\mathcal{L}_{\rm WTA}, supervises the candidate
closest to the logged trajectory. Joint training combines all four losses:
ℒ=ℒWTA+ℒscore+λfut​ℒfut+λbank​ℒbank,\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm WTA}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm score}+\lambda_{\rm fut}\mathcal{L}_{\rm fut}+\lambda_{\rm bank}\mathcal{L}_{\rm bank},
(7)
with λfut=1\lambda_{\rm fut}=1 and λbank=0.5\lambda_{\rm bank}=0.5.
We down-weight the bank loss to limit its influence on shared features while
retaining supervision from trajectories outside the generated candidate set.
3.5 Inertial Re-Ranking
We observe abrupt changes between plans selected in adjacent frames.
Framewise scoring can favor a high-scoring trajectory that is incompatible
with the previous choice; similar candidate scores can also make selection
sensitive to small changes. We therefore check each candidate against the previous plan πt−1\pi_{t-1} and use this compatibility to re-rank the current candidates.
For NAVSIM-v2, we instantiate the check with the benchmark’s two-frame
extended-comfort (EC) criterion (Cao et al., 2025). A deterministic
linear-quadratic-regulator (LQR) controller tracks the previous plan and each
current candidate with a kinematic bicycle model, producing ego-state rollouts.
After aligning the two rollouts on their common horizon, we compare their
acceleration, jerk, yaw-rate, and yaw-acceleration profiles. The candidate is
marked compatible when all four RMS differences fall below their respective
benchmark thresholds, giving the binary flag
ct,i=cEC​(πt−1,τt,i)∈{0,1}.c_{t,i}=c_{\rm EC}(\pi_{t-1},\tau_{t,i})\in\{0,1\}.
Logged human plans pass this check on 90.1% of adjacent frame
pairs (Appendix E.1).
Figure 5: Consecutive plans through a right turn.
Frames are 0.50.5 s apart. In (b), re-ranking selects a plan closer to the
preceding choice and the human trajectory. Both views share map coordinates.
The check uses only the previous plan, current candidates, and controller
rollouts, so it requires no future observations or labels at deployment.
Within each log, we process frames chronologically and carry the selected
plan forward. The first frame uses the learned score alone.
We add the compatibility result as a log-domain penalty to the learned utility:
Ψt,i≔log⁡(0.01+0.99​ct,i),it⋆∈arg⁡max1≤i≤N⁡[J^t,i+λstate​Ψt,i].\Psi_{t,i}\coloneqq\log(0.01+0.99\,c_{t,i}),\qquad i_{t}^{\star}\in\arg\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\bigl[\hat{J}_{t,i}+\lambda_{\rm state}\Psi_{t,i}\bigr].
(8)
With λstate=1\lambda_{\rm state}=1, compatible candidates receive no penalty and
retain their learned-score ordering; incompatible candidates receive
Ψt,i=−log⁡100\Psi_{t,i}=-\log 100. Re-ranking adds no learned parameters or training loss.
We report both framewise and re-ranked results on NAVSIM-v2.

4.1 Setup
We evaluate on all 12,146 NAVSIM test scenes using the NAVSIM-v1
PDM score (PDMS) (Dauner et al., 2024) and NAVSIM-v2 extended PDMS
(EPDMS) (Cao et al., 2025), defined in Appendix B.1.
Both metrics score the selected trajectory in the official simulator;
the learned utility only ranks candidates.
World4Scorer uses DrivoR’s (Kirby et al., 2026) training split,
LoRA-adapted DINOv2 encoder, generator, and candidate count;
we train for 25 epochs with AdamW, a peak learning rate of
2×10−42\times 10^{-4}, and a global batch size of 64. Linear warmup covers the
first 10% of training, then cosine decay. We select the main checkpoint
on the validation split.

4.3 ■\blacksquare Supervision and Re-Ranking (RQ2)
Realized-future targets.
Anchoring the executed state to its observed future raises PDMS from
93.01 to 93.96, whereas the same objective with
a current-frame target gives 92.70 (Table 2a).
The three runs share the architecture, seed, bank supervision, and last-epoch
evaluation; this single-seed gap is consistent with, but does not by itself
isolate, the targets’ temporal content.
Candidate–outcome pairing.
Shuffling bank outcome labels reduces ordering accuracy from 64.2%
to 55.6%; shuffling all labels gives 51.5%
(Table 2b). Selected PDM falls from 0.728
to 0.647 and 0.461. Each permutation preserves the
scene’s trajectories and label distribution but breaks their correspondence.
All three conditions train the same architecture on 512 scenes for 60 epochs
and evaluate on 511 scenes from disjoint logs. These controls
show that ranking requires the correct correspondence between trajectories and outcomes.
Inertial re-ranking.
With weights and candidates fixed, inertial re-ranking raises extended
comfort from 77.0 to 92.8 while
progress changes only from 90.0 to
89.8, yielding 1.6 EPDMS points
(Table 2c). In the right turn of Figure 5,
it favors trajectories compatible with the preceding choice.

5 Conclusion
World4Scorer scores candidates with a trajectory-conditioned predictor trained
by simulator outcomes and anchored by the observed future. Controlled
experiments show the value of correctly paired outcome labels and
realized-future targets, and state interventions show that selection depends on
candidate-specific states. The method achieves state-of-the-art NAVSIM-v2
performance and strong closed-loop driving results, and outcome-based scoring
also improves manipulation with a frozen world model. These findings support
grounding predictive states in decision outcomes.

All experiments use public benchmarks and their official evaluators
(NAVSIM-v1 and NAVSIM-v2, Bench2Drive, and OGBench-Cube).

A.2 Training and State Interventions
Trajectory and outcome losses.
Generated, executed, and bank queries share the predictor PP. The heads HH
score candidate states, while gg reads the executed state for visual prediction.
For generated candidates, ℒWTA\mathcal{L}_{\rm WTA} sums
absolute errors over coordinates, averages over poses, minimizes over
candidates, and then averages over scenes. We sum this loss for two targets:
the logged four-second trajectory and the same trajectory time-warped to
five seconds by cubic-spline resampling.

Appendix B Evaluation Protocols
We describe simulator labels and compare NAVSIM-v1 and NAVSIM-v2 objectives
with the model and candidate pools fixed, isolating the scoring rule.
B.1 Simulator-Based Training Labels
We obtain candidate labels by tracking each trajectory in the simulator and
measuring collision avoidance, progress, and other outcomes in three steps:
1.
Prepare the scene. A model-independent cache stores the initial
ego state, centerline, on-route lane IDs, drivable area, and the PDM planner’s
progress. It also stores 5151 occupancy timesteps as STRtree-indexed
polygons. Training and evaluation share this cache.
2.
Track the candidate. Generated trajectories are detached before scoring, transformed to the global frame, and resampled to
4040 poses at 0.10.1 s. An LQR controller tracks each
trajectory through a kinematic bicycle model, producing a
44-second ego-state sequence.
3.
Measure the outcomes. Collision avoidance, drivable-area
compliance, and driving direction use polygon queries against the cached maps.
The tracked ego-state sequence supplies time-to-collision, centerline progress, and comfort labels.
In the simulator’s aggregate score, the components combine in two groups. Collision avoidance and drivable-area
compliance multiply together, and their product both scales raw progress and
multiplies the aggregate score. For the v1 training-label scorer, progress,
time-to-collision, and comfort enter a weighted average whose weights match the
deployed NAVSIM-v1 tuple (1,1,0,5,5,2)(1,1,0,5,5,2){}; driving-direction compliance is
computed but has zero v1 weight.
Benchmark metrics.
NAVSIM-v1 reports the PDM score (Dauner et al., 2024),
PDMS=NC⋅DAC⋅5​TTC+5​EP+2​C12.\mathrm{PDMS}=\mathrm{NC}\cdot\mathrm{DAC}\cdot\frac{5\,\mathrm{TTC}+5\,\mathrm{EP}+2\,\mathrm{C}}{12}.
NAVSIM-v2 reports the extended PDM score (Cao et al., 2025),
EPDMS=NC⋅DAC⋅DDC⋅TLC⋅5​TTC+5​EP+2​HC+2​LK+2​EC16,\mathrm{EPDMS}=\mathrm{NC}\cdot\mathrm{DAC}\cdot\mathrm{DDC}\cdot\mathrm{TLC}\cdot\frac{5\,\mathrm{TTC}+5\,\mathrm{EP}+2\,\mathrm{HC}+2\,\mathrm{LK}+2\,\mathrm{EC}}{16},
which adds DDC and traffic-light compliance (TLC) as multipliers and lane keeping
(LK) and extended comfort (EC) to the weighted average; history comfort (HC)
replaces comfort (C). EC compares plans of consecutive frames. A term is
not penalized where the logged human trajectory also violates it.
Both metrics use the navtest scenes with non-reactive background agents.
Progress normalization.
Progress is normalized by a reference distance:
•
Training: the maximum of the candidate’s raw progress and the cached planner progress.
•
Evaluation: the official scorer normalizes progress over the proposals passed
to it, which are the PDM reference and the selected trajectory in our evaluation.
A training label depends only on its trajectory and scene, not on other candidates.
Selection and runtime.
At selection time, the predicted components are combined using
Equation 2. Collision avoidance, drivable-area compliance,
and driving direction contribute weighted log probabilities; the remaining
components contribute the log of their weighted sum.
The NAVSIM-v1 weights (1,1,0,5,5,2)(1,1,0,5,5,2){} assign zero weight to driving direction.
Generated candidates change at every step, requiring online labels for all
NN candidates per sample. Computing these labels on CPU with NumPy and
Shapely dominates training time.
B.2 Differences Between NAVSIM Evaluation Objectives
NAVSIM-v1 and NAVSIM-v2 select different candidates in
43% of scenes when applied to the same frozen
candidate pools of our planner. We use simulator sub-scores to isolate the evaluation
rule from prediction error. The two utilities have within-scene rank
correlation 0.69. Removing extended comfort raises
the correlation to 0.96 and reduces argmax disagreement to
3%. The V1-optimal candidate fails the EC check in 99.7% of disagreement scenes. Selecting the V2-optimal candidate lowers native NAVSIM-v1 PDMS by
1.3 points.
An exact additive decomposition attributes 98% of the utility gap between the V1 and V2 choices to EC, 3% to driving direction (weighted only in V2), and −1%-1\% to progress, time-to-collision, and comfort together
(progress alone: −0.7%-0.7\%). The V2-optimal candidate gains 50.4 EC
points and loses 2.7 progress points relative to the V1-optimal candidate.
Progress equals 1.01.0 for 43% of candidates, so pool-wide
percentiles from the sixtieth upward coincide. In 38% of scenes (57% in the most dynamic
quarter), no candidate is both top-quartile in progress and EC-passing.
The most dynamic quarter holds the scenes with the largest change between the
current and +2+2 s front-view DINOv2 features. This share becomes
30% at the median threshold and 23%
at a within-scene quartile. The within-scene progress–EC correlation is
+0.07.
The recomputed V1 selections differ from the benchmark results only on
39 fp32 ties, changing mean PDMS by 6×10−76\times 10^{-7}.
Recomputed EC matches the benchmark on all 10,040 adjacent pairs.
The benchmark omits this term for the remaining 2,106 frames, which lack a valid adjacent pair.
Appendix C Predictive Representations
We examine visual targets and predictions, illustrate the content retained
by pooled features, and connect learned candidate states to score differences.
C.1 Geometry of the Latent Prediction Target
We compare current and future DINOv2 features over 103,288
training frames. The current frame and its +2.0+2.0 s
future are highly similar (mean cosine 0.94, fifth percentile
0.80), indicating that persistent scene content dominates the target and that a
measurable temporal change remains. Both feature sets have
effective rank 49 of 384 dimensions.
Pairing each current frame with a future from another scene reduces mean
cosine to 0.649, close to the random-pairing baseline of 0.649; this shuffle
removes the pairing but keeps the target features. True and shuffled pairs are
readily distinguished (AUC 0.981, Cohen’s dd 2.84). Same-log
pairs are also less similar (0.76), so the true-pair advantage
is scene-specific.

C.4 Supervision Geometry and Candidate Comparisons
We expand the local model of Section 3.3 and prove
Proposition 1. The analysis follows the principle
that supervision should constrain differences relevant to candidate selection,
as in transductive experimental design (Fiez et al., 2019).

Relation to learning.
The result characterizes local sensitivity at a fitted network under
the same quadratic perturbation budget, not the optimization trajectory or
global planning performance after training.
Its quadratic form is a partial generalized Gauss–Newton metric for the BCE
and cosine terms, not the complete mixed training objective. No stationarity
of the individual loss terms is assumed; the analysis concerns local
first-order score sensitivity under this metric, and μ\mu restricts it to the
region where the linearization holds.
Target values affect the fitted network and hence its local geometry;
replacing a target at fixed parameters does not itself change the output
Jacobian. Section 4.3 evaluates the learning effects
of future targets and correctly paired candidate outcomes. The analysis
identifies a condition for complementary supervision without assuming
that latent-state coordinates or complete alternative futures are recovered.

D.2 Outcome-Based Scoring Inside the LeWM Planner
We use the official LeWM (Maes et al., 2026) implementation on
OGBench-Cube (Park et al., 2025). Its world model predicts the result of an action
sequence, and its planner searches for a sequence that reaches the goal.
We freeze the world model and add outcome-based scoring to this search.
The planner runs cross-entropy-method (CEM) search over 300 action
sequences of 5 blocks, each block 5
environment steps, and keeps the 30 best sequences at every
iteration. The goal is the expert’s cube pose 25 steps ahead,
and each episode has a budget of 50 environment steps. Success requires
placing the cube within 4 cm of this goal pose.
LeWM scores each sequence by the distance between its predicted final feature
and the goal feature, favoring smaller distances. Our scorer takes these two
features, their distance, and the action sequence, and predicts its outcome
score. At each CEM iteration, we standardize the negative distance and the learned score across candidates
and add the score to the distance with weight
λcube=0.3\lambda_{\rm cube}=0.3{}. This combined score selects the retained
sequences under the same world model and search budget.
The scorer learns from simulator outcomes of candidates collected by the
LeWM planner, using training seeds separate from evaluation. Both planners
use 10 CEM iterations, following LeWM’s Appendix D.
We choose λcube\lambda_{\rm cube} by success on tuning seeds 200–203;
these seeds are separate from the reported evaluation. Table 5
reports the mean over seeds 0–9 and 42, LeWM’s released
evaluation seed, each with 50 paired episodes. Each pair shares an initial state and goal.
Evaluation seeds.
All seeds use the same trained scorer, weight, and 10-iteration CEM budget.
LeWM succeeds on 379/550 episodes
(68.91%) and outcome-based scoring on
405/550 (73.64%), a gain of
+4.73 percentage points. The scorer rescues 39 episodes
and loses 13; success improves on 9 seeds, ties on
1, and decreases on 1. These runs vary the

Scorer architecture and training.
The scorer input concatenates the layer-normalized predicted final and goal features,
their difference and elementwise product, the log-transformed LeWM distance, and
the mean and standard deviation of the action sequence (819 dimensions).
A layer-normalized MLP with GELU activations and dropout 0.05 maps this input
through widths 256 and 128 to a scalar. The target is the negated
minimum cube-to-goal distance along each candidate’s simulator rollout,
standardized within its candidate group. The loss adds smooth-L1L_{1} regression and a
listwise cross-entropy term with weight 0.25 and softmax temperature 0.5.
Training candidates come from LeWM planning runs with seeds 100–107.
Episodes used in any evaluation are excluded, as are candidate groups whose
target standard deviation is below 10−410^{-4}. An episode-disjoint split
gives 2,982 training groups from 262 episodes and 725 validation groups from
65 episodes. We train for 80 epochs with AdamW (learning rate 10−310^{-3},
weight decay 10−410^{-4}, cosine schedule, 64 groups per batch) and keep the
epoch with the highest validation pairwise ordering accuracy (epoch 6,
counting from zero).

E.5 Closed-Loop Evaluation on Bench2Drive
We train the planner on the official 1000-clip subset and evaluate it in
CARLA using Bench2Drive (Jia et al., 2024). We evaluate an adapted system; published baselines are not retrained under
matched conditions. We initialize from a route-blind checkpoint trained on the
same clips, add the route-point input, and
use the simulator and route inputs below. Table 13
compares Driving Scores, and Figure 14 shows six completed
scenarios with camera frames every five seconds.
The CARLA ego input adds the route point 20 m ahead,
expressed in ego-frame meters, to the NAVSIM pose, velocity, acceleration,
and command inputs. The same coordinate conversion supplies this route
point during training and deployment.
We choose the distance by scanning 10–40 m over 15,589
frames from 60 training clips. At 20 m, the
point lies on the side of the turn for 97.4% of left-turn and
100.0% of right-turn frames, while the route ends before the point on
only 10.5% of frames. We then add two zero-initialized input
columns to the route-blind checkpoint and retain its training data, loss, and
schedule.
Training.
The model and losses follow NAVSIM with two changes.
Outcome labels are computed against the logged agent boxes and a drivable-area
raster of each clip, and extra negatives come from a trajectory vocabulary
instead of the CLOVER bank. The future loss has weight 0.1, and
its gradient into the predictor is scaled by 0.05. Both the
route-blind and the route-point model train for 15 epochs with AdamW
at a learning rate of 7.5×10−57.5\times 10^{-5} and 24 clips per GPU.
Two deployment rules need no retraining:
•
Command retention keeps a route node active until the vehicle
passes it, preserving the turn command through an intersection.
•
Route re-ranking measures agreement with the route polyline
within 30 m using an exponential kernel of average waypoint-to-route distance,
weighted toward later waypoints. We standardize this agreement across
candidates and add it to the learned utility with weight
4​σ4\sigma, where σ\sigma is the pool’s utility standard deviation.
We select the plan with the highest combined score.
