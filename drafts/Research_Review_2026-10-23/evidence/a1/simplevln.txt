                                                                                                    Abstract

                                                     Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) requires embodied agents to ground lan-
                                                     guage in visual observations and make long-horizon decisions. Existing methods of-
                                                     ten address these demands through task-specific architectures. As multimodal large
                                                     language models (MLLMs) become increasingly capable, an important question
                                                     remains: Have we truly unlocked the potential of native MLLMs for VLN before
                                                     introducing task-specific architecture? We present SimpleVLN, an architecture-
                                                     preserving framework developed through studies of navigation formulation, data
                                                     construction, and optimization. At the formulation level, SimpleVLN combines
                                                     single-turn decision inputs with fixed-budget global–local memory to efficiently
                                                     use navigation history without unbounded context growth, while action chunks
                                                     enable efficient output of multiple primitive actions per model invocation. For data
                                                     construction, we combine expert demonstrations with policy-dependent DAgger
                                                     experience and develop a scalable synthesis pipeline that generates large-scale tasks
                                                     for reinforcement learning. At the optimization level, a progressive IL–DAgger–
                                                     RL pipeline advances from action imitation to task-level outcome optimization.
                                                     Comprehensive experiments validate the effectiveness of these three aspects. We
                                                     instantiate SimpleVLN across multiple MLLM backbones and model sizes, demon-
                                                     strating its generality. SimpleVLN achieves 76.7% and 80.1% success rates on
                                                     the R2R-CE and RxR-CE splits, respectively, substantially outperforming existing
                                                     methods. These results show that the navigation capabilities of native MLLMs can
                                                     be effectively unlocked without task-specific architectural designs.


                                         1     Introduction
                                         Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) requires embodied agents to follow natural-language instruc-
                                         tions in unseen environments [1, 8], combining visual understanding, language grounding, spatial
                                         reasoning, and long-horizon decision making. Existing methods have advanced through task-specific
                                         designs for perception and spatial reasoning [22], graph-based modeling [3], and hierarchical or
                                         dual-system decision making [16]. These architectures are effective, while multimodal large language
                                         models (MLLMs) offer strong general-purpose visual understanding, reasoning, and instruction
                                         following [2]. However, the performance of augmented MLLM systems reflects both the backbone
                                         and task-specific components, leaving the capability of a native MLLM alone unclear. This raises
                                         an underexplored question: Have we truly unlocked the potential of native MLLMs for VLN before
                                         introducing task-specific architecture? To answer it, we investigate how far native MLLMs can be
                                         advanced for VLN without architectural modifications.

Preserving the architecture still requires adapting the MLLM to sequential navigation. We develop
SimpleVLN by studying three aspects: formulation, data construction, and optimization (Figure 1).
First, at the formulation level, we combine single-turn decision inputs with fixed-budget global–local
memory to prevent unbounded context growth while efficiently using navigation history. Action
chunking further enables efficient action output by predicting multiple primitive actions per model
invocation. Second, for data construction, we combine expert demonstrations with policy-dependent
experience collected through DAgger [10]. We further develop a scalable task-synthesis pipeline to
generate large-scale navigation tasks for reinforcement learning. Third, at the optimization level, we
investigate a progressive IL–DAgger–RL training pipeline. IL initializes the policy through action
imitation, DAgger adapts it to policy-induced states, and RL directly optimizes task-level outcomes,
progressively improving navigation accuracy.
Comprehensive experiments validate the effectiveness of these three aspects. We instantiate Sim-
pleVLN across multiple MLLM backbones and model sizes, demonstrating its generality. SimpleVLN
achieves 76.7% and 80.1% SR on the R2R-CE and RxR-CE val-unseen splits, respectively, substan-
tially outperforming existing methods. These results demonstrate that the navigation capabilities of
native MLLMs can be effectively unlocked without task-specific architectural designs.
Our contributions are threefold: (1) We introduce SimpleVLN, an architecture-preserving framework
that adapts native MLLMs to VLN through single-turn decision inputs, managed visual history, and
action-chunk prediction, without task-specific architectural modules. (2) We construct training data
from expert demonstrations, policy-dependent DAgger experience, and scalable RL task synthesis, and
develop a progressive IL–DAgger–RL pipeline for improving navigation accuracy. (3) We instantiate
SimpleVLN across multiple backbone families and model sizes, achieving strong performance on
R2R-CE and RxR-CE, while extensive experiments validate the effectiveness of its formulation, data
construction, and optimization.


2     Related Work

Vision-and-Language Navigation. Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) requires agents to
follow natural-language instructions grounded in visual observations [1, 8]. While early VLN operates
over predefined discrete viewpoints, VLN-CE [7] extends navigation to continuous environments
with low-level actions.
Multimodal Large Language Models for VLN. Recent VLN methods leverage multimodal large
language models (MLLMs) for vision–language understanding [25, 4, 24], while introducing stream-
ing contexts [17], 3D geometry encoders [22], dual-system architectures [16, 6, 18, 12], or explicit
chain-of-thought reasoning [9, 14] for long-horizon navigation. In contrast, SimpleVLN directly
adapts a pretrained MLLM without architectural modifications, retaining compatibility with existing
MLLM inference frameworks.

Training Paradigms for Navigation. Imitation learning trains navigation policies from expert
trajectories [17, 28], but suffers from distribution shift under policy execution. DAgger [10] mitigates
this issue using supervision on model-induced states [17, 22], while RL [28, 5] directly optimizes
task-level outcomes. SimpleVLN combines them in a progressive IL-DAgger-RL pipeline from
expert supervision to task-completion optimization.


3     SimpleVLN

3.1   Overview

SimpleVLN directly adapts off-the-shelf MLLMs [13, 2] to VLN without architectural modifications.
We study native-MLLM navigation from three perspectives: navigation formulation, data construction,
and a progressive optimization strategy that shifts from action imitation to outcome-driven learning
through environment interaction.
As illustrated in Figure 2, given a natural-language instruction I, at each primitive environment step
k, the agent observes an egocentric RGB image xk and executes an action uk from
             A = {F ORWARD(0.25 m), T URN L EFT(15◦ ), T URN R IGHT(15◦ ), S TOP}.
The episode terminates when S TOP is executed or the primitive-action budget Smax is reached, and
succeeds if the agent stops within 3 m of the target [7].

3.2   Decision-Level Navigation Formulation

We formulate navigation around model decision steps rather than primitive environment steps. At
each decision, SimpleVLN reconstructs a bounded context from the instruction, current observation,
and sampled history, and uses the native MLLM to predict navigation actions. We first describe the
construction of the decision context and then introduce the output action representation.
Single-turn decision input. At each model decision, we construct an independent input rather than
append the latest observation and predicted action to the preceding dialogue. Let t = 1, 2, . . . index
model decisions, and let nt denote the number of primitive actions executed before decision t, with
n1 = 0. The current observation is therefore ot = xnt . The input consists of the instruction I, the
current observation ot , and a sampled visual history Ht ; previous dialogue turns and past actions are
not included. We denote the resulting visual context by st = (ot , Ht ).

Action chunking. On the output side, a single model decision can specify multiple primitive actions.
Rather than predicting one primitive action per decision, we follow NaVIDA [30] and predict an
action chunk at , defined as an ordered sequence of primitive actions. Let Kt denote the number of
primitive actionsin the chunk; then at = (unt , . . . , unt +Kt −1 ), and the navigation policy predicts
at ∼ πθ · | I, st = πθ · | I, ot , Ht , where πθ denotes the native MLLM navigation policy. The
environment executes the actions in at sequentially, producing an observation after each. These
observations are retained for later history construction. The MLLM is queried again when the chunk
finishes, unless S TOP or the primitive-action budget terminates the episode.

3.3     Navigation Data Construction

We construct training data for each stage of post-training. Expert demonstrations provide action
supervision, while DAgger adds policy-dependent states encountered during model execution. At
the RL stage, reusing the same episodes used for supervised learning would not expand the task
distribution or provide genuinely new interaction experience. We therefore synthesize new navigation
tasks in the training scenes.


                                                                                                     4
Expert demonstration coverage. We pool trajectories from R2R [1], RxR [8], and ScaleVLN [15]
to broaden expert behavior coverage. To match the decision-level inference interface in Section 3.2,
each trajectory is converted into supervised samples with (I, st ) as input and the corresponding
expert action chunk a⋆t as the target, where st = (ot , Ht ).
Policy-dependent experience via DAgger. Broader expert demonstrations do not eliminate the
distribution gap between expert trajectories and states visited by the current policy. We therefore use a
deviation-based DAgger [10] strategy. We compare the current policy trajectory with the ground-truth
(GT) trajectory: when the deviation is large, we select the expert trajectory; otherwise, we retain
the policy trajectory. Selected trajectories are converted into decision-level supervised samples and
aggregated with the demonstrations.
Task synthesis for RL. Because the R2R, RxR, and ScaleVLN training episodes are already used
for supervised learning, we synthesize new RL tasks in their training scenes rather than reuse the
annotated episodes, excluding val-unseen scenes. For each source, we sample start–goal pairs in
different rooms from the navigation graph, with goals associated with semantically labelled objects.
An MLLM generates navigation instructions from reference-path waypoints describing turns, rooms,
salient objects, and egocentric views. The reference path supports task construction rather than
providing an action target.

3.4   Progressive Optimization

Figure 3 summarizes the three-stage optimization of SimpleVLN. First, imitation learning uses expert
demonstrations to learn navigation actions. Next, DAgger-based fine-tuning adapts the policy to states
encountered during its own execution while retaining action-level supervision. Finally, reinforcement
learning optimizes task-level outcomes through environment interaction on synthesized episodes,
shifting the learning signal from demonstrated actions to outcome feedback.
Imitation learning from expert actions. Let D denote a supervised dataset of tuples (I, st , a⋆t ),
where a⋆t denotes the target action chunk for decision state st . We minimize   the negative log-
likelihood of the complete target chunk: LSFT = −E(I,st ,a⋆t )∼D log πθ a⋆t | I, st . This objective
teaches the policy to reproduce demonstrated action chunks, learning from expert supervision.
Policy-dependent supervised adaptation. We next train on the aggregation of demonstration and
DAgger data from Section 3.3. The objective LSFT is unchanged, while D additionally includes
target action chunks a⋆t derived from the selected policy-dependent trajectories.
Outcome optimization through reinforcement learning. Because navigation is ultimately evaluated
by task completion rather than agreement with a particular action sequence, we optimize task-level
outcomes on the synthesized navigation tasks. Using the synthesized episodes described in Section 3.3,
the policy interacts with the environment and sequentially predicts decision-level action chunks,

Here, T is the actual number of model-level decisions before the episode terminates, distinct from
the primitive-action budget Smax . We use a binary task-level reward to make task completion the

completes the task is rewarded. Training therefore progresses from expert action supervision, through
policy-dependent supervised adaptation, to episode-level task-completion feedback.

4.3             Formulation

We investigate the navigation formulation introduced in Section 3.2. Unless otherwise specified, these
experiments use Qwen3-VL 4B fine-tuned on R2R and evaluated on the R2R-CE val-unseen split.
Action chunking. Table 3a evaluates action chunking as an alternative to primitive-action prediction.
Action chunking raises SR from 45.6 to 53.6, an 8.0-point gain, supporting it as an effective output
representation beyond reducing model invocation frequency.
Single-turn decision input. Table 3b replaces accumulated multi-turn dialogue with a reconstructed
single-turn input and sampled visual history, raising SR from 35.7 to 53.6. Although multi-turn
dialogue can retain a fuller interaction history, continually appending observations and actions creates
an overly long context that current MLLMs cannot effectively exploit for navigation.
Observation sampling. Table 3c examines which observations to retain. Sampling only chunk
boundaries yields 46.3 SR, retaining all primitive-step observations yields 51.9, and uniformly
sampling eight global history frames yields 53.6. The eight-frame budget covers global history only,
excluding local history and the current observation. These results favor compact, uniformly sampled
global history over retaining every frame.
Memory budget. Figure 4 tests whether increasing the global or local history budget improves
navigation accuracy. With N = 1, SR peaks at M = 8 and then declines as the global budget grows,
suggesting that excessive global history introduces redundant or less relevant observations that dilute
useful context. With M = 8, retaining one local observation gives the highest SR, indicating that
additional nearby frames provide largely redundant information. We therefore use M = 8 and N = 1:
up to eight global and one local history frame, plus the current observation.

4.4             Data Construction

We investigate the effects of the training data constructed in Section 3.3. All experiments in this
subsection use Qwen3-VL 4B and report results on the R2R-CE and RxR-CE val-unseen splits.
Expert demonstration coverage. Table 4 tests whether broadening expert demonstration coverage
improves navigation. Adding RxR to R2R raises SR from 53.6 to 56.7 on R2R-CE and from 50.3 to
54.5 on RxR-CE. Including ScaleVLN further increases SR to 57.9 and 55.3, respectively. These
results show that combining R2R and RxR provides clear in-domain gains, while ScaleVLN further
improves generalization to unseen environments.
Policy-dependent experience via DAgger. We next assess whether policy-dependent data improves
over the combined expert demonstrations. Adding DAgger yields the largest gains in Table 4:
7.5 SR points on R2R-CE and 8.1 on RxR-CE, reaching 65.4 and 63.4. This shows the value of
complementing static demonstrations with trajectories collected by the learned policy.
Task resynthesis for RL. Table 5 evaluates RL tasks synthesized from the R2R, RxR, and ScaleVLN
training scenes. These names denote scene pools for synthesis, not reused annotated episodes. All
variants start from the same IL–DAgger checkpoint and use GRPO [11] with batch size 32 and eight
rollouts per prompt. Each pool improves SR over the pre-RL baseline, while their mixture performs
best, reaching 70.1 on R2R-CE and 71.0 on RxR-CE.

4.5   Optimization

We investigate how RL configurations affect navigation accuracy and how the model’s navigation
behavior evolves during training. Table 6 uses Qwen3-VL 4B and a mixture of synthetic episodes
constructed from the R2R, RxR, and ScaleVLN training scenes.
Reward design. Under GRPO with batch size 32 and eight rollouts per prompt, we compare SPL
and binary rewards. Both assign zero to failures; successful episodes receive either the SPL score or
a reward of one. The binary reward raises SR from 68.9 to 70.1 on R2R-CE and from 67.7 to 71.0
on RxR-CE, supporting simple task-completion feedback.
RL configuration. With the success reward fixed, DAPO [20] reaches 71.5/73.7 SR, compared with
70.1/71.0 for GRPO. Both ablations use a batch size of 32, compared with 256 for the main-results
configuration in Section 4.1. These results favor the evaluated DAPO configuration, although we do
not attribute the gain solely to the optimization algorithm.
RL training dynamics. Figure 5 shows that, despite fluctuations, rollout reward and evaluation SR
trend upward, while the average number of action-chunk decisions per trajectory gradually decreases.
This suggests that RL improves navigation performance while also increasing decision efficiency.

4.6   Real-world Experiments

We further evaluate SimpleVLN in real environments using a Daystar quadruped robot. The model
runs onboard on an NVIDIA Jetson Thor GPU. The robot is equipped with an Intel RealSense D450
RGB-D camera for egocentric perception. We only use RGB as our observations. Figure 6 shows that
SimpleVLN successfully follows multi-step instructions involving forward movement, turns, room
entry, and landmark-based stopping, while maintaining coherent instruction-following behavior over
extended trajectories.

5     Conclusion
We presented SimpleVLN, a framework for adapting native MLLMs to VLN. SimpleVLN combines
single-turn decision inputs, global–local memory, and action chunking with training data constructed
from expert demonstrations, policy-dependent DAgger experience, and synthesized RL tasks. A pro-
gressive IL–DAgger–RL pipeline further shifts learning from action imitation to task-level outcome
optimization. SimpleVLN achieves strong navigation performance, demonstrating that native MLLM
navigation capabilities can be effectively unlocked without task-specific architectural designs.
