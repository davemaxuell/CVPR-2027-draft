1  INTRODUCTION

Vision-language navigation (VLN) requires an embodied agent to ground natural-language instruc-
tions in visual observations and execute sequential actions to reach a target. This task begins with
R2R (Anderson et al., 2018) and has expanded to longer-range navigation in RxR (Ku et al., 2020),
goal-oriented settings such as REVERIE (Qi et al., 2020) and SOON (Zhu et al., 2021), and con-
tinuous navigation in VLN-CE (Krantz et al., 2020). These increasingly challenging settings place
greater demands on language grounding, spatial reasoning, and long-horizon decision making. Ac-
cordingly, research has progressed from improving navigation architectures and learning strate-
gies (Chen et al., 2021; 2022a; Wang et al., 2023b; An et al., 2024) to scaling instruction-trajectory
supervision through automatic data synthesis (Chen et al., 2022b; Wang et al., 2023c; 2025b).

Scalable data synthesis allows navigation agents to learn from large amounts of unlabeled trajecto-
ries that would otherwise lack language supervision. Early methods use speaker models and back-
translation to generate instructions for additional trajectories (Fried et al., 2018; Tan et al., 2019),
while later work substantially scales this paradigm with larger environments and stronger instruc-
tion generators (Chen et al., 2022b; Wang et al., 2023c; 2025b;a). Despite this progress, current
pipelines still leave substantial room for improvement. Most methods learn instruction generation
and navigation separately, while existing dual-learning approaches typically rely on task-specific ar-
chitectures (Wang et al., 2022; 2023a), limiting the benefit of bidirectional language-action learning.
In addition, synthetic instructions are commonly generated by a fixed model, and the navigation pol-
icy is updated only after a large set of trajectories has been annotated. As the policy improves, the
generated supervision can therefore become increasingly mismatched with the model being trained.
These limitations call for a learning process that jointly exploits the two directions of language-
action grounding while keeping newly generated supervision synchronized with the current policy.

This connection is particularly natural in navigation. More generally, complementary capabilities
can benefit each other when one provides feedback for the other. An unlabeled trajectory can first
be described as a route instruction and then executed again from its starting point, forming two
complementary directions of language-action grounding. Instruction generation maps visual-action
experience to language, while navigation maps language and visual observations back to actions. A
faithful instruction should capture the navigation task well enough to guide the agent to the intended
goal efficiently, making navigation execution a direct way to evaluate the generated supervision.
When both capabilities are handled by the same evolving model, execution feedback can filter and
improve language supervision, which in turn provides better data for subsequent navigation learning.

Based on this observation, we introduce RSI-Nav, a data-centric recursive self-improvement frame-
work for navigation via policy-synchronized language-action dual bootstrapping. RSI-Nav uses a
shared model to unify instruction following and generation for dual bootstrapping. We first apply
supervised fine-tuning to establish basic capabilities for navigation and trajectory annotation. The
model then progressively processes unlabeled trajectories in small shards. For each shard, the model
generates instructions and executes them. The resulting rollouts assess whether generated instruc-
tions can guide the current model to the intended goal efficiently, and only qualified instruction-
trajectory pairs are retained. The model is then immediately updated before the next shard. As a
result, the updated model generates and evaluates the rollouts used in subsequent training, rather
than relying on data produced by a fixed annotator at the beginning of the process. This shard-
wise update keeps the generated supervision closely aligned with the evolving policy and forms a
recursive process in which better models produce better training data for subsequent improvement.

Beyond language-action dual learning, we also incorporate spatial understanding into the same
model, as spatial reasoning is crucial for interpreting routes and making navigation decisions (Ma
et al., 2022; Cheng et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025).  Joint training with spatial-understanding
data strengthens this capability and in turn benefits navigation. We evaluate RSI-Nav on continu-
ous vision-language navigation, trajectory-to-instruction generation, and spatial understanding. On
R2R-CE (Krantz et al., 2020) and RxR-CE (Ku et al., 2020), RSI-Nav consistently improves nav-
igation performance over its supervised initialization. The evolved model also maintains strong
spatial understanding, with modest improvements on all four evaluated benchmarks over its super-
vised initialization, while its generated instructions provide stronger training utility for subsequent
navigation. We further analyze the self-improvement process, examining policy synchronization,
dual learning, action-conditioned instruction generation, and spatial supervision. These analyses
explain why the proposed process improves both generated supervision and the navigation policy.
Generally, our contributions can be summarized as follows:

• We present RSI-Nav, a data-centric recursive self-improvement framework that unifies instruction
  following, instruction generation, and spatial understanding within a single model, where dual
  language-action learning provides complementary supervision to improve navigation.
• We obtain large-scale execution-verified instructions paired with source trajectories as well as a
  strong unified model. To our knowledge, RSI-Nav is the first navigation framework to combine
  unified language-action dual learning with policy-synchronized recursive self-improvement.
• We demonstrate strong navigation performance on VLN-CE benchmarks, including leading suc-
  cess rates among the compared methods, while maintaining strong spatial understanding. Analy-
  ses examine key components of the pipeline to explain how and why RSI-Nav improves.

2  RELATED WORK

Vision-and-Language Navigation.  Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) requires agents to
follow natural-language instructions and navigate to target locations in visual environments. Exist-
ing scenarios include navigation with detailed action instructions (Anderson et al., 2018; Ku et al.,
2020; Thomason et al., 2020), remote object navigation with coarse-grained instructions (Zhu et al.,
2021; Qi et al., 2020), and navigation in continuous environments (Kolve et al., 2017; Krantz et al.,
2020; Hong et al., 2022).  Substantial progress has been made through vision-language pretrain-
ing and history modeling (Hao et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021; Guhur et al., 2021), explicit map
construction and planning (Chen et al., 2022a; Wang et al., 2023b; An et al., 2024), and modern
vision-language models that directly predict navigation actions (Zhou et al., 2024; Zheng et al.,
2024; Zhang et al., 2024). However, the high cost of human annotation remains a major bottleneck,
motivating automatic data augmentation and synthesis. Early methods use speaker models and back-
translation (Fried et al., 2018; Tan et al., 2019), while later work exploits large-scale environments
and synthetic instructions to construct millions of instruction-trajectory pairs (Chen et al., 2022b;

Wang et al., 2023c; 2025b; Li et al., 2025). Recent studies further highlight spatial understanding,
including situated 3D reasoning and spatial memory (Yang et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2025).

Complementary Learning and Mutual Bootstrapping.  Learning complementary capabilities
together provides supervision and supports joint improvement across multiple abilities. In machine
translation, dual learning couples forward and backward translation to provide richer mutual feed-
back (He et al., 2016). Similar ideas also appear in mathematical reasoning, where a language model
acts as both solver and verifier (Weng et al., 2023; Lightman et al., 2023), with recent work jointly
training answer generation and self-verification in a single model (Zhang et al., 2025a). These meth-
ods broadly share the principle that complementary capabilities can provide supervision or feedback
for one another. In VLN, instruction following and generation form a similar bidirectional language-
action relationship. Prior work realizes this through separate speaker and follower models, where
generated instructions mainly serve as augmentation or scoring signals, or through task-specific
architectures and consistency objectives (Fried et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2022; 2023a).

Recursive Self-Improvement.  Recursive self-improvement (RSI) broadly studies how an AI sys-
tem can contribute to improving its subsequent versions.  In the data-centric setting, RSIBench-
Data (Meng et al., 2026) frames this as turning model failures and checkpoint feedback into better
training data and, in turn, better models. Related approaches generate, filter, or verify model outputs
for subsequent training in language reasoning (Zelikman et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2023; Xiong et al.,
2025; Yuan et al., 2024) and embodied agents (Bousmalis et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2025). In VLN,
SRDF (Wang et al., 2025b) iteratively refines synthetic instructions, self-correction methods (Yu
et al., 2026) mine failure trajectories, and SID-VLN (Li et al., 2025) reuses successful exploration
trajectories. However, these methods generally separate data generation and model updates into
relatively coarse rounds, allowing supervision to lag behind the evolving policy.


3  METHODOLOGY

RSI-Nav learns from unlabeled navigation trajectories through policy-synchronized language-action
dual bootstrapping. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the framework begins with supervised initialization and
recursively improves both the model and its generated supervision. We first introduce the contin-
uous VLN setting, followed by a shared model that unifies instruction following, instruction gen-
eration, and spatial understanding in Sec. 3.1.  Sec. 3.2 presents the core data-centric recursive
self-improvement process, where the current policy generates instructions, executes them to assess
goal completion and path efficiency, retains qualified samples, and updates itself before processing
the next trajectory shard. Finally, Sec. 3.3 describes how we curate supervision for initialization and
recursive evolution, including spatial-data replay and newly generated instruction-trajectory pairs.

Preliminary.  We consider vision-language navigation in continuous environments, where an agent
follows a natural-language instruction through low-level interaction with the environment.  At
step t, the agent receives an egocentric RGB observation ot and selects an action at ∈A =
{FORWARD, LEFT, RIGHT, STOP}. A navigation trajectory is represented as

                                     τi = (o1, a1, o2, a2, . . . , oT , aT ) ,                               (1)
where T denotes the trajectory length. Each trajectory starts from an initial state sstarti    and ter-
minates at a target location defined by its final state. Given a natural-language instruction xi, the
agent is required to reach the corresponding target efficiently. We use the term policy-synchronized
rather than strict on-policy learning. The source trajectories remain offline, while instruction gen-
eration and execution-based evaluation are repeatedly refreshed by the current model, reducing the
gap between generated supervision and the evolving policy, as detailed in Sec. 3.2.

tinguished by task-specific prompts, without separate generators, verifiers, or output heads. We
formulate the two complementary language-action tasks and present spatial understanding below.

Spatial Understanding.  We further incorporate spatial-understanding training data, which pro-
vide an important scaffold for embodied decision making and navigation (Gong et al., 2026; Team

3.2  POLICY-SYNCHRONIZED RECURSIVE SELF-IMPROVEMENT

With the shared instruction-following and instruction-generation model defined above, RSI-Nav
recursively improves on a large corpus of unlabeled continuous trajectories. We partition the corpus
into small, non-overlapping trajectory shards U = {B1, B2, . . . , BK} and process each trajectory
once. After each shard, the model is immediately updated before generating new supervision for the
next one. This fine-grained update keeps data generation and execution-based evaluation close to
the current policy, rather than relying on a fixed model over the entire corpus.

Generate, Execute, and Filter.  For each trajectory τi ∈Bk, the model generates an instruction ˆxi
from the visual-action trajectory and then follows ˆxi from the original start state. The endpoint of τi
serves as the navigation target. This execution tests whether the generated instruction can guide the
current model to the intended destination efficiently. We retain a sample based on goal completion
and path efficiency. An execution is considered successful if its final position is within 3 m of the
target. We further use SPL to measure navigation efficiency and define the acceptance indicator as
                                   qi = I [di < 3 m ∧SPLi ≥τSPL] ,                             (4)

where di is the geodesic distance from the final position to the target and τSPL is a fixed efficiency
threshold. Only samples satisfying both criteria are retained for subsequent training.

Dual Bootstrapping and Shard-Wise Update.  The filtering stage determines which generated
instructions are reliable enough for training. For each accepted pair (τi, ˆxi), we retain the original
trajectory τi.  Since ˆxi is generated from τi, successful and efficient execution toward the same
goal provides an execution-grounded check that the instruction is executable and consistent with the
navigation task represented by the source trajectory. We therefore retain the original trajectory as the
supervision target rather than the executed trajectory, avoiding the accumulation of execution errors
and the cost of repeatedly re-rendering continuous trajectories in Habitat. Keeping the trajectory
fixed also focuses recursive improvement on the generated language supervision.

Each accepted pair provides supervision for both complementary directions. For instruction fol-
lowing, ˆxi is paired with the observation histories and original actions along τi; for instruction
generation, (eVτi, Aτi) is paired with ˆxi. For shard Bk, these accepted samples form DIFk  and DIGk  ,
respectively. During the shard-wise update, we mix them with spatial-understanding data replayed
from supervised initialization. The overall training objective is
                   Lk = LIF DIFk + LIG DIGk  + Lspa (Rspak  ) .                        (5)
Here, LIF and LIG follow the objectives defined in Sec. 3.1, while Lspa denotes the corresponding
loss on spatial-understanding samples. This decomposition only distinguishes the three supervision
sources; in practice, all samples are mixed and jointly optimized with a single SFT objective. The
resulting θk is immediately used to process the next trajectory shard.

Recursive Self-Improvement.  The updated model θk immediately processes the next shard Bk+1.
Refreshing instruction generation and execution-based filtering ensures that new supervision is pro-
duced by the latest model rather than a fixed policy. Repeating this cycle allows the model and
its generated supervision to co-evolve across shards, while replayed spatial-understanding data help
maintain the capability acquired during SFT initialization. The full evolution yields a stronger uni-
fied model and a large collection of execution-verified instruction-trajectory pairs, detailed in Sec. 4.

3.3  TRAINING DATA AND SUPERVISION

RSI-Nav is initialized with multi-source supervision for instruction following, instruction gener-
ation, and spatial understanding. We use continuous navigation data from SRDF (Wang et al.,
2025b), EnvDrop (Tan et al., 2019), ScaleVLN (Wang et al., 2023c), R2R (Anderson et al., 2018),
and RxR (Ku et al., 2020), and construct corresponding instruction-generation data by reversing
the trajectory-instruction pairs. Since SRDF trajectories are originally discrete, we convert them
into continuous trajectories for training, with details in Appendix D. We also incorporate spatial-
understanding data from VSI-590K (Yang et al., 2026), 546K samples from LLaVA-3D (Zhu et al.,
2024), and MindCube (Yin et al., 2025). All data are mixed for standard supervised fine-tuning,
with the same spatial data replayed during recursive evolution. Starting from unlabeled continuous
trajectories, RSI-Nav generates about 2.5M instruction-following samples and 1.1M instruction-
generation samples through policy-synchronized recursive improvement.

4  EXPERIMENTS

Implementation Details.  RSI-Nav is built on Qwen3.5-9B and trained in two stages on 64 GPUs.
First, we perform supervised fine-tuning for one epoch on the multi-source data described in Sec. 3.3,
using a learning rate of 5×10−6, a global batch size of 128, and AdamW optimization. We then con-
duct recursive self-improvement on the unlabeled trajectory corpus, partitioned into non-overlapping
shards of 16,384 trajectories. After each shard, qualified instruction-trajectory pairs are immediately
used for model updating together with spatial-understanding replay before processing the next shard.

Benchmarks and Evaluation Metrics.  We evaluate navigation on R2R-CE (Krantz et al., 2020)
and RxR-CE (Ku et al., 2020) using their validation-unseen splits. Following prior work, we report
Navigation Error (NE), Oracle Success Rate (OSR), Success Rate (SR), and Success weighted by
Path Length (SPL) on R2R-CE, and additionally nDTW on RxR-CE for trajectory alignment. For

4.2  ABLATION STUDIES

How Does Policy Synchronization Affect Learning?  We study the update granularity on a fixed
subset of 64K unlabeled trajectories using 64 GPUs, where candidate denotes the number of trajec-
tories processed per GPU before each policy update. As shown in Tab. 3, 256 candidates perform
best, improving SR and SPL most significantly. With only 64 candidates, performance also drops,
suggesting that each update changes the policy too little for successive policies and their generated
data to differ meaningfully. In contrast, 1,024 candidates process the entire subset before updating,
approaching the conventional offline setting and enlarging the policy-data gap. The intermediate
granularity therefore provides a better balance between meaningful policy evolution and timely su-
pervision refresh. Based on this ablation, we use 256 candidates per GPU, corresponding to a global
shard size of 16,384 trajectories on 64 GPUs, for full-scale recursive evolution.


Does Dual Learning Benefit Navigation?  We examine whether instruction generation provides
useful supervision for instruction following. As shown in Tab. 2, adding VA2L data improves SR
from 44.7% to 48.8% and SPL from 38.6% to 41.0% over navigation-only training, confirming that
trajectory annotation benefits navigation. Tab. 5 further shows that joint instruction following and
generation substantially improves navigation while causing minor changes in instruction-generation
metrics. This indicates that the two tasks can be jointly optimized with little loss in annotation qual-
ity, while the complementary trajectory-to-language supervision provides clear gains for navigation.

Are Actions Important for Dual Learning?  Under the same visual input, VA2L consistently
outperforms V2L, improving SR from 47.0% to 48.8% and SPL from 38.8% to 41.0%. Although
instruction generation can rely on visual observations alone, incorporating actions establishes a
stronger dual relation between action-to-language generation and language-to-action navigation.
This indicates that action information helps more effectively transfer instruction-generation super-
vision back to navigation; we further examine this effect from the generation side next.


How Does Joint Training Affect Instruction Generation?  For RSI-Nav, we use the jointly
trained VL2A-VA2L generator evaluated in Tab. 5. We examine the reverse benefit of dual learning
from instruction following to instruction generation. Prior methods are often evaluated in discrete
Matterport3D settings with panoramic observations, whereas RSI-Nav operates in continuous VLN-
CE using low-resolution Habitat observations; their NLP scores thus serve only as reference rather
than strictly comparable baselines. NLP metrics also mainly measure similarity to human-written
instructions and may not reflect downstream training utility (Gadre et al., 2024). We therefore an-
notate the same trajectories with different generators and train identical navigation models on the
resulting data. As shown in Tab. 4, although SRDF scores higher on most NLP metrics, RSI-Nav
yields substantially better downstream navigation, improving SR from 36.8% to 41.3% and SPL
from 30.4% to 35.1%. Together with the bidirectional-training results in Tab. 5, these results show
that joint VL2A-VA2L training benefits navigation while preserving instruction-generation quality,
and that the resulting RSI-Nav generator yields stronger downstream utility than prior work.


Does Spatial Understanding Benefit Navigation?  Adding spatial-understanding supervision on
top of VA2L further improves all navigation metrics, reducing navigation error while increasing
success rate and efficiency. The gains support the role of spatial relations and viewpoint reasoning
in navigation, highlighting spatial understanding as complementary supervision.


Limitations.  RSI-Nav still relies on simulated environments and an offline trajectory pool, while
execution-based filtering incurs considerable computational cost. Although we provide qualitative
real-world demonstrations, systematic robustness across diverse real-world environments, unseen
domains, and substantially longer-horizon navigation remains to be established.

5  CONCLUSION

We introduce RSI-Nav, a data-centric recursive self-improvement framework for navigation via
policy-synchronized language-action dual bootstrapping. RSI-Nav unifies instruction following,
generation, and spatial understanding in one shared policy, turning unlabeled trajectories into super-
vision through instruction generation, execution-based filtering, and shard-wise updates. By refresh-
ing supervision with the latest model, RSI-Nav maintains policy-synchronized learning while jointly
training instruction following and generation. Experiments on R2R-CE and RxR-CE demonstrate
strong navigation improvements, while spatial-understanding evaluations show modest gains over
supervised initialization and generated instructions provide useful downstream navigation supervi-
sion. Ablations examine the effects of update granularity, dual-task training, action conditioning,
and spatial supervision. Overall, RSI-Nav shows that large-scale unlabeled navigation trajectories
support model improvement through generated and filtered supervision, providing a scalable direc-
tion for self-improving embodied navigation. We discuss limitations and future work in Appendix H.

We evaluate navigation using NE, SR, OSR, SPL, and nDTW. For episode  i,  let Pi =
(pi,0, . . . , pi,Ti) denote the executed trajectory, gi the goal, and Ri = (ri,1, . . . , ri,Mi) the refer-

navigation mesh and 1[·] the indicator function.

H  LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

RSI-Nav’s self-improvement is constrained by the capabilities of its current policy. Since the same
model generates instructions and executes them for verification, the resulting supervision may re-
inforce existing biases.  Instructions that are valid but difficult to execute may also be rejected,
potentially favoring easier trajectories and limiting further improvement. Future work could explore
difficulty-aware data selection, adaptive filtering, and complementary verification signals to retain
challenging examples while controlling supervision quality.

Execution-based filtering also provides an incomplete measure of instruction quality.  Reaching
the intended destination efficiently does not necessarily imply that every landmark description or
spatial relation is correct. Such semantic errors may persist in accepted examples and propagate
through subsequent updates. Future work could combine trajectory-level verification with grounded
semantic checks and targeted human evaluation to better assess instruction fidelity and understand
how errors accumulate across self-improvement rounds.
