Language-Conditioned World Modeling for Visual Navigation Yifei Dong1,∗, Fengyi Wu1,∗, Yilong Dai1, Lingdong Kong2, Guangyu Chen1, Yetong Sha1, Qiyu Hu1, Feng Liu3, Siyu Huang4, Qi Dai5, Zhi-Qi Cheng1,† 1University of Washington, 2National University of Singapore, 3Drexel University, 4Clemson University, 5Microsoft Research ∗Equal contribution, listed in random order, †Corresponding author. Goal-conditioned visual navigation has been a long-standing testbed for embodied AI. We study a natural language-conditioned variant, language-conditioned visual navigation (LCVN), in which an embodied agent must follow a natural language instruction given only an initial egocentric observation. Without access to goal images, the agent must rely on language to shape its perception and continuous control. We introduce the LCVN Dataset, a benchmark of 39,016 trajectories and 117,048 human- verified instructions spanning diverse environments and instruction styles. Building on this benchmark, we study two complementary paradigms: (i) latent-imagination policy learning, in which a diffusion- based world model (LCVN-WM) imagines future observations and an actor–critic agent (LCVN-AC) learns its policy entirely within the imagined latent space; and (ii) unified autoregressive prediction, in which a single multimodal backbone (LCVN-Uni) jointly predicts actions and observations in one forward pass over a shared token sequence. Experiments show that two paradigms offer complementary strengths: latent imagination produces more temporally coherent rollouts, whereas unified prediction generalizes better to unseen environments. Targeted ablations further isolate the contributions of language guidance, conditioning signals, and instruction style, clarifying when language grounding versus dynamics modeling is the performance bottleneck. Together, these findings position LCVN as a testbed for studying how language, imagination, and decision-making interact in embodied agents. These goal-conditioned policies are typically trained on offline datasets of recorded egocentric trajectories and evaluated by their ability to predict future actions from a single starting observation, a protocol that has enabled large-scale data sharing across robots and environments (Shah et al., 2022). Visual goals, however, can be a limiting interface for many real-world settings, since users rarely have a photograph of the intended destination at hand. A more natural and information-rich alternative is natural language, which can convey not only a target location but also intermediate landmarks, directional cues, and broader contextual constraints (Zhang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2025). A brief instruction such as “After passing a glass wall on your left, turn left at the corner.” often suffices to guide action even in unfamiliar places, and humans seamlessly integrate such guidance with what they see. Building embodied agents that can do the same, grounding language effectively in perception and continuous control, remains a core open challenge (Parker-Holder et al., 2024; Ball et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2025, 2026). These limitations call for methods that better align language grounding, neural simulation, and policy learning. To study this problem, we introduce language-conditioned visual navigation (LCVN) task (Fig. 1), in which an agent receives a natural language instruction and a single egocentric starting observation, and predicts the entire future trajectory toward the destination it describes. Following the trajectory-prediction protocol of prior goal-conditioned navigation work (Shah et al., 2023; Sridhar et al., 2024; Shah et al., 2022), training and evaluation operate on offline datasets of recorded trajectories, providing a reproducible testbed in which different solution approaches can be compared on identical inputs without dependence on interactive simulators. To support rigorous and systematic evaluation, we further construct LCVN Dataset, a large-scale corpus of language-annotated trajectories spanning diverse environments and instruction styles. Building on this benchmark, we instantiate and systematically compare two paradigms that couple language grounding, future-state prediction, and policy learning in qualitatively different ways. The first paradigm (Fig. 2), latent-imagination policy learning, trains a language-conditioned diffusion world model LCVN-WM that imagines future latent observations, paired with an actor–critic agent LCVN-AC that learns its policy entirely within the imagined latent space via intrinsic rewards. The second paradigm (Fig. 3), unified autoregressive prediction, fine-tunes a language-conditioned autoregressive world model LCVN-Uni that jointly predicts actions and observations in a single forward pass over a shared token sequence. Extensive experiments show that LCVN agents outperform strong baselines on both navigation and imagination benchmarks, with latent-imagination policy learning producing more temporally coherent rollouts and unified autoregressive prediction generalizing better to unseen environments. We provide experiments and ablations that characterize the behavior and generalization of both paradigms on the LCVN task, positioning LCVN as a shared testbed for studying how language, imagination, and decision making shape embodied behavior. 2 Related Work Visual Navigation spans goal-conditioned and free-exploration settings, with goal-conditioned navigation long regarded as a core robotics challenge (Sridhar et al., 2024; Shah et al., 2023; Mirowski et al., 2016; Chaplot et al., 2020; Fu et al., 2022; Frey et al., 2023; Pathak et al., 2018). In this setting, an agent receives context and target images and must generate trajectories to reach the goal (Shah et al., 2023; Sridhar et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2026b). Early systems combined neural SLAM with analytical planning (Chaplot et al., 2020), while later work learned direct mappings from egocentric observations to actions with reinforcement learning (Chen et al., 2019; Lillicrap et al., 2015; Haarnoja et al., 2018). More recent foundation-model approaches (Shah et al., 2023; Sridhar et al., 2024; Shah et al., 2022) train policies on offline datasets of recorded trajectories to predict future actions from a single starting observation and a goal specification, a protocol that has enabled large-scale data sharing across heterogeneous robots and environments. However, these approaches typically specify the goal as a visual image, underutilizing the intent and contextual cues that language can convey (Zhang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2025). In parallel, language-guided navigation has been studied in ObjectNav (Yadav et al., 2023; Deng et al., 2025), VLN (Anderson et al., 2018; Krantz et al., 2020), and REVERIE (Qi et al., 2020), which evaluate agents in simulators where observations are received at every step. LCVN occupies a distinct point: unlike discrete graphs (Anderson et al., 2018; Qi et al., 2020) or simulator-bound continuous control (Krantz et al., 2020; Yadav et al., 2023), it inherits the offline trajectory-prediction protocol of goal-conditioned navigation with language goals over recorded real-robot trajectories, complementing closed-loop evaluation and scaling to heterogeneous real-world data across diverse platforms and environments. World Models in Navigation. World models can be understood as learned neural simulators of environment dynamics (Ding et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024; Chu et al., 2026): rather than relying on hand-built physics or rendering engines, they acquire a predictive model of how observations evolve under actions directly from data. This perspective has driven a rapid evolution from recurrent latent dynamics (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2019, 2020, 2023) to Transformer-based (Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024; Karypidis et al., 2024; Baldassarre et al., 2025) and diffusion-based architectures (Brooks et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2025; Valevski et al., 2024; Bar et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025), with LLM-driven variants further modeling dynamics via language prompting (Zhao et al., 2025; Xing et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Within navigation, two design philosophies have emerged. Policy-centric methods (Shah et al., 2022, 2023; Sridhar et al., 2024) map observations directly to actions, treating future-state prediction as implicit. Model-centric methods explicitly predict future observations for planning, instantiated as GAN-based imagination in PathDreamer (Koh et al., 2021), diffusion video rollouts in NWM (Bar et al., 2025), and unified autoregressive prediction in UniWM (Dong et al., 2025a). Yet these works largely decouple imagination from policy learning: NWM omits language entirely, PathDreamer’s auxiliary inputs hinder generalization (Lin et al., 2024), and UniWM’s interleaved training and inference are inefficient. How language grounding, neural simulation, and policy learning should be jointly designed under a single task remains underexplored. LCVN provides such a setting, in which we instantiate two paradigms: latent-imagination policy learning, pairing a diffusion-based world 3

model LCVN-WM with an actor–critic agent LCVN-AC trained in its latent space; and unified autoregressive prediction, where a single multimodal backbone LCVN-Uni jointly predicts actions and observations. 3 LCVN: Language-Conditioned Visual Navigation In this section, we formalize the language-conditioned visual navigation task, a natural-language variant of goal-conditioned visual navigation. An embodied agent begins navigation with an egocentric RGB observation os and receives a natural language instruction I = ⟨w1, w2, . . . , wn⟩, consisting of a sequence of words. The agent’s goal is to generate a sequence of navigation actions AT = {ˆa1, ˆa2, . . . , ˆaT } that successfully guides it to the destination described by the instruction. Each action ˆat can take the form of a continuous control command (ut, ϕt). In detail, the planar displacement ut ∈R2 represents forward, backward, and lateral motion, together with a yaw rotation ϕt ∈R. The action sequence terminates with a null action indicating arrival at the goal location. Following the trajectory-prediction protocol of prior goal-conditioned navigation work (Shah et al., 2023; Sridhar et al., 2024; Shah et al., 2022), the agent predicts this entire action sequence from the initial observation os and the instruction I, and is evaluated on offline datasets of recorded trajectories by comparing the predicted trajectory against the recorded ground truth. The instruction I serves as a persistent contextual condition that modulates the agent’s policy throughout the episode. 3.1 LCVN Dataset To support research in language-conditioned visual navigation, we construct LCVN, a large-scale multi-style instruction dataset built by augmenting five heterogeneous robotic trajectory datasets with human-verified language instructions. We use Go Stanford (Hirose et al., 2018), ReCon (Shah et al., 2021), SCAND (Karnan et al., 2022), and HuRoN (Hirose et al., 2023) for training and in-domain evaluation, covering complementary aspects of real-world navigation (e.g., open-world settings in ReCon, socially compliant behavior in SCAND), and reserve TartanDrive (Triest et al., 2022) as an unseen split for zero-shot evaluation, since its off-road driving scenes differ markedly from the indoor and structured outdoor environments seen in training, providing a strong distribution shift for testing generalization. Trajectories are standardized by normalizing per-frame displacement by each dataset’s average step size, filtering out backward movements and trajectories shorter than three steps (Bar et al., 2025; Sridhar et al., 2024), and segmented into semantically coherent sub-scenes using Qwen-3.5-9B (Team, 2026). We adopt a seeded few-shot annotation strategy: our annotation team first manually writes a seed set of high-quality instructions, which then serves as the few-shot prompt context for Qwen-3.5-9B to generate instructions at scale (Liu et al., 2025), followed by human verification and refinement. Alongside the egocentric video, we additionally render a top-down 2D trajectory map as visual input, which disambiguates global heading changes from local motion noise. Each trajectory receives instructions in three styles: Concise (essential directional cues), Intricate (rich scene descriptions), and Landmark-based (anchored to environmental landmarks), enabling systematic evaluation of language grounding under varying levels of detail rather than a single uniform style (Kolagar and Zarcone, 2024). The final LCVN dataset contains 39,016 trajectories and 117,048 instructions, partitioned into training (28,813), val seen (3,602), val unseen (1,500, solely from TartanDrive), and test (5,101, from both TartanDrive and other sources). Beyond its primary application in language-conditioned visual navigation, LCVN dataset may also support related tasks like style-aware instruction generation (Kong et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025) and multimodal understanding (Tong et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). To verify annotation quality, a human evaluation on 640 randomly sampled trajectories yields an overall quality score of 5.2/6.0, confirming instruction quality across all three styles. Language Conditioning. To enable language conditioning, we encode the instruction I into Iclip using a frozen CLIP text encoder (Radford et al., 2021). The LDiT architecture integrates a multi-head cross-attention layer within each DiT block, positioned between the self-attention and feed-forward layers, as shown on the right side of Fig. 2. This design enables flexible interaction between visual latents and the instruction embeddings Iclip, which are directly fed into the cross-attention mechanism, thereby promoting semantic consistency between states and goals. Meanwhile, we also include action and time conditioning within the conditioning mechanism following the settings in (Bar et al., 2025). The navigation action ˆat is encoded by first applying sine–cosine positional encoding to each scalar, followed by a MLP, and then concatenating results into a single vector. A similar procedure is applied to encode relative timeshift ts and diffusion timestep. The final vector is obtained by summing all three embeddings, which is then used in an AdaLN layer to generate scale and shift parameters that modulate outputs of Layer Normalization and intermediate activations in each attention block. Data Preprocessing. Each training sample consists of (ot, at, os, I) paired with two targets, the next action ˆat+1 and the next observation ˆot+1 (see Appendix for prompt design). This eliminates need for separate sample types for planning and world modeling, and streamlines the training pipeline. Multimodal Tokenization. We employ three tokeniz- ers (Dong et al., 2025a) to couple multimodal inputs: a VQ image tokenizer (Gafni et al., 2022) for visual obser- vations ot and os, a BPE tokenizer (Team, 2024) for in- structions I and text prompts, and a bin tokenizer (Dong et al., 2025a) for actions at. The resulting token se- quences are fed to a causal Transformer that jointly models planning and imagination. Joint Training Objective. At each training step, the model produces logits for both action bins and visual tokens, enabling end-to-end joint optimization: Ljoint = Lplan + λLimagine, (11) where Lplan is the discretized bin token loss (Dong et al., 2025a) for action prediction, Limagine (Dong et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2025) is the reconstruction loss for predicted future observations, and λ balances the two. This shared objective lets the same backbone learn representations that serve both planning and visual prediction. Inference. At test time, LCVN-Uni generates the next action and observation in a single forward pass, avoiding alternating substeps used in (Dong et al., 2025a). TartanDrive’s off-road driving scenes differ markedly from the other four sources in terrain, camera dynamics, and visual appearance, providing a strong distribution shift for zero-shot evaluation. The test split intentionally blends held-out trajectories from all five sources so that a single evaluation simultaneously measures in-domain proficiency and out-of-domain generalization. To standardize trajectories from heterogeneous sources, we normalize per-frame displacement by each source’s average step size to yield an embodiment-agnostic continuous control space, filter out backward movements and trajectories shorter than three steps following (Bar et al., 2025; Sridhar et al., 2024), and segment each visual stream into semantically coherent sub-scenes using Qwen-3.5-9B (Team, 2026). A.2 Seeded Few-Shot Annotation Pipeline Our annotation team first manually writes a seed set covering the three target styles, distributed across diverse environment types and trajectory complexities (straight, single-turn, multi-turn). Annotators are instructed to write naturally and idiomatically, with attention to stylistic markers that distinguish the three styles: terse imperatives for Concise, descriptive subordinate clauses for Intricate, and landmark-anchored prepositional phrases for Landmark-based. This seed set serves as the linguistic anchor for all subsequent generation. For each target trajectory, we render a top-down 2D map alongside the egocentric video. The map plots the agent’s planar trajectory using local odometry, with the start point marked, the heading direction indicated by an arrow at each waypoint, and turning angles annotated; sampled camera frustums convey the egocentric viewpoint orientation. Without this 2D map as visual input, the VLM frequently misinterprets terrain-induced camera shake or local sidesteps as deliberate global heading changes, producing instructions like “turn right” for trajectories that are globally straight. The 2D map disambiguates global heading from local motion noise, substantially improving geometric correctness of generated instructions. We then construct a style-specific few-shot prompt for each trajectory by sampling seeds matched to the target style, environment type, and complexity, and pass this prompt along with the target trajectory’s egocentric frames and 2D map to Qwen-3.5-9B. We generate three candidate instructions per trajectory (one per style). All generated instructions are subsequently reviewed by our annotation team, who cross-reference them with the corresponding video and 2D map and check for geometric correctness, landmark accuracy, style adherence, and naturalness. Annotators correct geometric or landmark errors and rewrite instructions that drift from the target style. The final dataset thus combines human authorship at the seed stage, VLM scaling at the generation stage, and human verification at the refinement stage, rather than the simple “machine-generated then verified” pipeline that motivates concerns about linguistic homogeneity. A.3 Human Quality Evaluation To quantitatively assess the quality of the final instructions, we conduct a human evaluation on a random subset of the dataset. We sample 640 trajectories roughly balanced across the five sources and three instruction styles, and recruit volunteer evaluators from our research group who are not involved in the annotation pipeline. Each evaluator watches the egocentric video, views the corresponding 2D trajectory map, and rates the paired instruction on five dimensions using a 1–6 Likert scale, where 6 indicates the instruction is indistinguishable from expert human-written ones: (1) Trajectory–Instruction Alignment, whether the instruction accurately describes the path including turns, distances, and stopping point; (2) Style Adherence, whether the instruction conforms to its target style without drifting into characteristics of another; (3) Geometric Correctness, whether directional references and spatial relations are consistent with egocentric viewpoint; (4) Landmark Accuracy, whether mentioned visual landmarks are present and correctly localized; and (5) Naturalness & Fluency, whether the instruction reads as natural language a human guide would produce. Each trajectory–instruction pair is rated by three evaluators, and scores are averaged across evaluators and dimensions. The overall mean score across all 640 samples is 5.2 out of 6.0, indicating that the vast majority of instructions are rated close to expert human quality. These results confirm that the combination of seeded few-shot generation, 2D map conditioning, and human verification yields instructions of consistently high quality across all three styles. 12

First, following prior goal-conditioned navigation work (Shah et al., 2023; Sridhar et al., 2024; Shah et al., 2022), we adopt an offline trajectory-prediction protocol, which enables reproducible comparison across heterogeneous real-robot data but does not capture closed-loop behaviors such as recovery from execution errors or interaction with dynamic agents; closed-loop validation on physical platforms is a natural complement. Second, all instructions in the LCVN dataset are written in English and follow three pre-defined styles (Concise, Intricate, Landmark-based); extending the benchmark to multilingual instructions and to noisier, more naturalistic phrasings that arise in real human–robot interaction is a promising direction. Third, our zero-shot evaluation relies primarily on TartanDrive’s off-road driving scenes as the unseen split, which provides a strong distribution shift in terrain and embodiment but leaves additional axes of generalization open for future exploration. E Broader Impacts LCVN targets language-conditioned visual navigation, which has natural applications in assistive robotics, service robots, and accessible human–robot interfaces, where users may prefer natural language over visual goals or low-level commands. We expect direct negative societal impacts to be limited: the world models studied in this work generate short-horizon egocentric navigation imagery rather than photorealistic media of identifiable individuals, and the underlying datasets are publicly released robotic trajectories without personally identifiable content. As with other navigation and generative-modeling research, downstream misuse in surveillance or in fabricating first-person footage is conceivable; we encourage subsequent work building on LCVN to follow responsible release practices and to consider provenance and access controls when deploying derived systems. 19
