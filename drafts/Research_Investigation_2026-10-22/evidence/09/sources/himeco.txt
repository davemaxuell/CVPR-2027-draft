14                                                    Abstract
                    Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have made substantial progress in spatial reasoning, and self-
                 evolving agents further extend these capabilities to embodied environments by learning from interac-
                     tions. However, current self-evolution remains particularly challenging in embodied spatial tasks, com-
                  plex and multi-step decisions from partial visual evidence making it difficult to determine why an execu-
                   tion fails. The failure may stem from misperception, insufficient exploration, or incorrect planning, yet[cs.AI]                  execution feedback alone rarely reveals which component is responsible. As a result, simply accumu-
                    lating interaction experience can reinforce ineffective strategies. To address this, we propose HiMeco, a
                   brain-inspired self-evolving agent for embodied spatial intelligence, inspired by metacognitive monitor-
                  ing and hippocampal memory consolidation. Through Hippocampal-Metacognitive Reflection, HiMeco
                 diagnoses failures in perception-exploration-planning (PEP), then compares execution attempts to de-
                    rive reusable strategies and guidance for avoiding recurring errors. It also abstracts interaction programs
                   into parameterized skills, using evidence contracts and execution-based validation to determine their
                   retention and reuse. These strategies and skills guide subsequent interactions, with memory updated
                 only between rounds and model parameters kept frozen. HiMeco achieves state-of-the-art performance
               on ESI-Bench, and it also improves accuracy on EmbSpatial-Bench by 3.94 percentage points without
                   evolution.


       1  IntroductionarXiv:2610.20312v1
         Embodied agents operating in complex physical environments require robust spatial intelligence, the abil-
            ity to not only perceive visible geometry but also reason about hidden structures and topologies [Majumdar
            et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2025, Gong et al., 2026, Feng et al., 2026]. While Vision-Language Models (VLMs)
         have demonstrated remarkable zero-shot reasoning capabilities, their spatial understanding remains funda-
          mentally constrained by passive observation [Yang et al., 2025, Gong et al., 2026, Hong et al., 2026]. In
           real-world scenarios riddled with non-line-of-sight (NLOS) occlusions and unobservable physical dynamics,
           passive VLMs struggle to perform deep spatial reasoning without actively interacting with the environment
           to uncover latent topologies [Feng et al., 2026, Majumdar et al., 2024, Hong et al., 2026].
              Existing research on enhancing spatial reasoning in VLMs broadly falls into two paradigms based on
           physical feedback availability: static perception [Cheng et al., 2024, Song et al., 2025, Zhu et al., 2025]
         and dynamic interaction [Ziliotto et al., 2025, Hong et al., 2026]. The former, whether through offline
          optimization (e.g., SFT or RL) or static prior injection from expert modules (e.g., 3D/depth estimation and

prompt engineering), treats the environment as a passive and fixed observation space.  Lacking closed-
loop interaction, it relies heavily on explicit in-view visual cues, suffering from cognitive blind spots under
NLOS occlusions and hidden topologies [Feng et al., 2026, Hong et al., 2026]. In contrast, the dynamic
interaction paradigm incorporates physical environment feedback into planning, enabling agents to actively
resolve local ambiguities [Ziliotto et al., 2025, Kim et al., 2025]. However, extending self-evolving agent to
embodied spatial tasks introduces a critical bottleneck. Prior work on self-evolving agent often focused on
tasks where the causes of failure are relatively easy to identify. While in embodied spatial tasks, agents must
make complex multi-step decisions based on partial and dynamically acquired visual evidence in continuous
3D environments subject to physical noise. These conditions make failures difficult to diagnose, while
feedback across interaction rounds offers limited guidance on how to revise the underlying strategies [Li
et al., 2024, Duan et al., 2025, Qi et al., 2026, Ju et al., 2026]. This ambiguity hinders agents from reliably
assessing which strategies are effective and identifying productive directions for further adaptation. As a
result, agents may reinforce flawed experiences and update strategies in the wrong direction, causing errors
to accumulate over successive evolution rounds [Ju et al., 2026].
   Remarkably, animals can efficiently distill effective strategies from experience to solve embodied spatial
tasks in unfamiliar environments. This adaptive ability of biological brains is supported in part by interac-
tions between the hippocampus and prefrontal metacognitive circuits [Liao and Losonczy, 2024, Jensen
et al., 2024, Fleming, 2026]. In contrast, existing embodied spatial intelligence, whether static (e.g., via
parameter updates or external tools) or dynamic self-evolving systems, struggle to deliver accurate spa-
tial reasoning during active exploration [Yang et al., 2025, Feng et al., 2026, Hong et al., 2026]. Because
they cannot determine whether failures stem from noisy perception, suboptimal exploration, or improper
planning [Li et al., 2024, Ju et al., 2026]. This makes it difficult to translate interaction feedback and ac-
cumulated experience into consistent behavioral improvements, leaving errors unresolved across successive
interactions [Ju et al., 2026, Zhang et al., 2026b, Tian et al., 2026]. These limitations motivate Hippocampal-
Metacognitive Reflection, which couples failure diagnosis with memory consolidation to guide future inter-
actions. Inspired by these neurobiological functions, we hope to achieve a brain-inspired self-evolving agent
equipped with multi-turn Hippocampal-Metacognitive reflection for resource-efficient and adaptive embod-
ied spatial intelligence.
    Translating this brain-inspired concept into practice presents two challenges. The first is adaptive seman-
tic abstraction: deciding what to retain from repeated interactions and how broadly it should apply [Zhang
et al., 2026b, Shu et al., 2026, Xu et al., 2026]. Across interaction and evolution rounds, a parameter-frozen
agent must turn individual attempts into reusable strategies and executable skills. Overly specific knowledge
transfers poorly, while overly general knowledge loses the detail needed for reliable execution [Wang et al.,
2025e, Zhang et al., 2026b]. The second is reliable failure attribution [Li et al., 2024, Ju et al., 2026]. An
incorrect answer alone does not reveal whether the agent misinterpreted its observations, explored insuffi-
ciently, or executed a flawed plan. Without this distinction, the agent may retain unreliable procedures or
reinforce ineffective strategies.
   To address these challenges, we propose HIMECO, a brain-inspired self-evolving agent for embodied
spatial reasoning. It consolidates interaction experience into a strategy bank E and a library of parameterized
executable skills K. Evidence contracts and invocation-based validation govern which skills are retained,
promoted, or suspended across evolution rounds. A perception-exploration-planning (PEP) diagnostic mod-
ule U uses execution traces, task outcomes, and privileged simulator metadata to assess failures across these
three dimensions after execution. These diagnostics guide candidate comparison and reflection, yielding
reusable strategies and negative constraints for subsequent interactions. Model parameters remain frozen,
and both memories are updated only between rounds.
   HIMECO alternates between online inference and offline evolution. During inference, the frozen VLM
generates programs conditioned on retrieved experiences and skills, while evidence contracts check the ob-
servations and intermediate outputs used for reasoning. Memory remains fixed throughout each interaction

2  Related Work

Embodied Spatial Intelligence.  Benchmarks span static visual reasoning, such as EmbSpatial-Bench [Du
et al., 2024], and interactive evaluation, such as ESI-Bench [Hong et al., 2026], which requires acquiring
evidence through actions. Existing approaches use task-specific fine-tuning [Song et al., 2025, Wang et al.,
2025a], explicit 3D memory [Zeng et al., 2026, Zheng et al., 2025], or interactive exploration [Ziliotto
et al., 2025, Hong et al., 2026] to improve spatial understanding. Training-free alternatives organize frozen
models within external structure: TANGO composes navigation and perception primitives into task-specific
programs without additional training [Ziliotto et al., 2025], NeSy-Spatial abstracts tool interactions into
typed and composable spatial skills that self-evolve with use [Tian et al., 2026], and Spatial Memory Agent

Experience-to-Skill Abstraction.  Self-evolving agents adapt by storing and reusing interaction experi-
ence, often without updating model parameters [Wang et al., 2025e, Liu et al., 2025, Xu et al., 2025, Zhang
et al., 2026a]. VOYAGER accumulates an ever-growing library of executable code skills under an auto-
matic curriculum [Wang et al., 2023], and later work evolves increasingly broader portions of the agent
system: SHAPER co-evolves reusable skills and a context-code harness around a frozen planner [Wang
et al., 2026a], MetaSkill-Evolve makes skill improvement itself recursive by evolving meta-skills on a
slower timescale [Wang et al., 2026d], and the Darwin Gödel Machine applies open-ended evolution to
an archive of self-modifying agents [Zhang et al., 2026c]. Complementarily, Training-Free GRPO distills
group-relative feedback into experiential token priors rather than parameter updates [Youtu-Agent Team,
2025]. These frameworks refine textual guidance or execution programs through reflection, with applica-
tions in code generation and web navigation.In embodied environments, this process is complicated by per-
ceptual errors,insufficient exploration, and planning failures, and skill-aware reflectionhas begun to separate
flawed skill content from execution lapses[Ju et al., 2026].Without reliable attribution and execution-based
validation, agents risk retaining ineffective strategies or unreliable skills.Existing skill-evolution frameworks
rarely distinguish these causes before updating memory.


Brain-Inspired Cognitive Evolution.  Brain-inspired approaches draw on mechanisms such as metacog-
nitive monitoring and hippocampal memory consolidation to support adaptive behavior [Fleming, 2026,
Huang et al., 2024]. Related work explores dual-system reasoning [Shi et al., 2026], cognitive maps [Liao
and Losonczy, 2024], episodic memory [Shu et al., 2026, Zheng et al., 2025], and predictive world models

3  Method

3.1  Problem Setup

Figure 2 summarizes the interaction, diagnosis, and memory-update process of HIMECO. In round r, a
frozen planner generates programs from input x and memory Mr = (Er, Kr), comprising a strategy bank
and a skill library. The diagnostic module U evaluates their execution trajectories Tr to guide the round-end
update Mr+1 ←Update(Mr, Tr, U).
   Each episode has input x = (o0, q), where o0 contains the initial RGB image and pose, and q is a spatial
query. The frozen VLM planner Φ generates P ∼Φ(·  | x, A, Mr) using observation, navigation, and
reasoning primitives A. The program, a JSON abstract syntax tree (AST), calls primitives and branches on
new observations; episode-local working memory stores observations, intermediate states, and evidence.
   Execution yields τ = (P, {(ak, ok)}Kk=1, ˆy, V ), where ak, ok denote the k-th action and resulting obser-
vation, K counts actions within budget Tmax, ˆy is the answer, and V records evidence checks. Given ground
truth y, correctness rtask, evidence eligibility ℓ, and learning success s are

                     (1[ContractValid(τ, q)],
          rtask(τ) = 1[ˆy = y],   ℓ(τ) =                             s(τ) = rtask(τ) ℓ(τ).          (1)
                                             1,

Here, 1[·] is the indicator function, equal to one when its condition holds and zero otherwise. ContractValid(τ, q)
indicates whether the recorded evidence passes the final contract for query q. rtask(τ) measures answer cor-
rectness, ℓ(τ) indicates evidence eligibility, and s(τ) indicates eligibility as a successful memory example.

3.3  Strategy Experience Bank

Individual trajectories contain instance-specific details that transfer poorly across tasks. The strategy bank
Er records when a strategy applies, how to acquire evidence, and which failures to avoid. Let Cr(x) denote
the candidates for episode x in round r. Their diagnostic information forms Br(x) = {z(τ) : τ ∈Cr(x)},
where z(τ) summarizes execution, PEP scores, the primary diagnostic label, relative advantage, evidence
eligibility, and learning success. Updates come from three sources. When the eligible-success set C+r (x) =
{τ ∈Cr(x)  : s(τ) = 1} is nonempty, we select the candidate with the highest advantage, breaking ties
by fewer actions. We abstract its use of primitives into a reusable strategy, excluding answer values and
episode-specific coordinates.
    Relative advantages provide limited guidance when candidates share the same failure. We therefore flag
candidate collapse when |Cr(x)| ≥2, Pτ∈Cr(x) s(τ) = 0, and all candidates return the same answer.
Deterministic rules produce corrective guidance, such as acquiring another viewpoint or adding a visual
check, annotated with the primary diagnostic label.
   An LLM reflector Ψ compares candidates within each bundle to identify useful strategies and lessons
from failures.  Its updates, ∆Ereflr  = Ψ({Br(x)}x), are combined with updates from selected successful
candidates ∆Echampr     and shared failures ∆Efailr    :

                          Er+1 = Apply Er, ∆Echampr       , ∆Efailr   , ∆Ereflr      .                            (6)

Here, Apply adds, revises, or removes entries at the round boundary under the memory capacity limit. The
strategy bank may retain episode-specific entries during adaptation, while final-round strategy retrieval uses
only task-level guidance.


3.4  Composite Skills

The skill library K stores reusable routines for acquiring evidence. Since recurring patterns may still be
unreliable, abstraction is followed by execution-based validation. Skills replace object-specific inputs with
parameters, retain control flow and evidence checks, and exclude answer submission. For a program pattern
p, let Xp contain episodes with occurrences eligible for abstraction, and X p+ ⊆Xp those with eligible

each skill k, let Nuse, Nexec, Nvalid, Nsucc count episodes with invocation, valid execution, contract-valid

prior strength. Skill quality is

                      Q(k) =wsρ(Nsucc, Nvalid) + wcρ(Nvalid, Nexec)

where the nonnegative weights sum to one and ˜η(k) is the prior-smoothed median action efficiency over
successful contract-valid invocations. Validation checks execution reliability, evidence compliance, and task
success to determine reuse eligibility; retrieval prioritizes lifecycle status, empirical success, and episode
support.


3.5  Interaction and Evolution Protocol

Each round uses a fixed memory snapshot. For each episode, candidates are evaluated under an experience-
only condition and, when available, a trial-skill condition. After all candidates finish, PEP diagnostics guide
strategy updates, while execution outcomes update skill support and reuse eligibility. The updates take effect
in the next round, allowing experience from one episode to guide other episodes in the same subtask. After
R rounds, the memory is frozen, and only validated skills are reused.

4  Experiments

4.1  Experimental Setup

Datasets. We evaluate active and passive embodied spatial reasoning on ESI-Bench [Hong et al., 2026]
and EmbSpatial-Bench [Du et al., 2024], respectively. ESI-Bench, built on OmniGibson, assesses spatial
reasoning through interaction with the environment. We evaluate repeated-episode adaptation on 208 fixed
episodes spanning nine tasks, with identical episodes and initial scenes across our settings. Cross-episode
transfer is examined separately through disjoint source-target examples. For EmbSpatial-Bench, we ran-
domly sample 300 questions per spatial relation category, totaling 1,800 questions across six categories. Its
fixed visual inputs lack the interaction feedback required for PEP failure attribution. We therefore evaluate
HIMECO without evolution against API inference using the same backbone and inputs. Sampling details
are provided in the appendix.
   Models. We use GPT-5.6-Terra and Gemini-3-Flash with frozen parameters and shared task inputs,
tool interfaces, and scoring criteria. On ESI-Bench, we also evaluate a Passive Single-View baseline using
only the initial image.  Experiments run on a server with eight NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs, accessing
VLMs through APIs. We retain execution traces for analysis and provide prompts, tool configurations, and
hyperparameters in the appendix.

4.2  Results

Results on ESI-Bench.  Table 1 compares HIMECO with existing methods reporting ESI-Bench results,
covering VLM baselines, interactive agents like ParallelWorld [Chen et al., 2026], and self-evolving agents
such as SHAPER [Wang et al., 2026a]. HIMECO achieves state-of-the-art average accuracy of 69.19%
on the evaluated ESI-Bench tasks. Relative to the corresponding active baselines, accuracy improves from
56.89% to 69.19% with GPT-5.6 and from 54.74% to 62.51% with Gemini-3-Flash, yielding gains of 12.30
and 7.77 percentage points without task-level degradation. The largest gains occur on Spatial Distance
(+25.0 points) and Physical Contact (+24.0) with GPT-5.6, and Spatial Distance (+15.0) and Connectiv-
ity (+11.1) with Gemini-3-Flash. These improvements demonstrate the effectiveness of diagnosis-guided
evolution for active embodied spatial reasoning, although Counting w/ Occlusion remains challenging.

Ablation Study.  Table 2 shows that full HIMECO achieves the best or tied-best accuracy across all four

5  Conclusion

This paper presented HIMECO, a brain-inspired self-evolving agent for embodied spatial intelligence. Its
Reflection mechanism couples PEP failure diagnosis module with the consolidation of reusable strategies
and executable skills. Evidence contracts and execution-based validation govern skill retention and reuse,
enabling adaptation without updating model parameters. HIMECO achieves SOTA performance on ESI-
Bench and improves accuracy on EmbSpatial-Bench by 3.94 percentage points without evolution.

B  Algorithm Overview

Algorithm 1 gives the four-round schedule. Algorithms 2 and 3 update the strategy bank E and skill library
K. Section F defines diagnosis U, which runs once per completed trajectory.
  We use the main-text notation Mr = (Er, Kr), Cr(x) for candidate trajectories, Tr for their union, and
Br(x) for a diagnostic bundle. For the reported schedule, r = t + 1, where t = 0, 1, 2, 3 is the displayed
execution round. Thus, M1 is empty and M4 is frozen for Round 3. Here cr is the candidate count. In the
reported evaluation, the planner generates each candidate from the round’s memory snapshot.

Algorithm 1 Four-round execution and memory updates

Require: Episodes D; frozen planner Φ and reflector Ψ; diagnosis U; task budgets Tmax(q)
Ensure: Round-0 and Round-3 results; frozen memory M4
 1: M1 ←(∅, ∅)
 2: for r = 1, 2, 3, 4 do
 3:    Fix Mr for this round; Tr ←∅
 4:     cr ←1 if r ∈{1, 4}, otherwise 3
 5:     for episode x = (o0, q) ∈D do
 6:        Cr(x) ←∅
 7:        for i = 0, . . . , cr −1 do
 8:            Select candidate memory and eligible skills (Section C.3)
 9:           Generate and validate P using Φ and the selected memory
10:           Reset x; execute P within Tmax(q) to obtain τ
11:            Evaluate rtask(τ), ℓ(τ), and s(τ) (Section E.2)
12:          Compute diagnostic scores and f ∗(τ) with U (Section F)
13:           Cr(x) ←Cr(x) ∪{τ}
14:       end for
15:       Compute R(τ) and Adv(τ) within Cr(x) (Section F.4)
16:       Form Br(x); Tr ←Tr ∪Cr(x)
17:    end for
18:       if the round is incomplete then
19:        Stop without advancing the memory checkpoint
20:    end if
21:       if r < 4 then
22:       Update Er using Algorithm 2
23:       Update Kr using Algorithm 3
24:       Save Mr+1 ←(Er+1, Kr+1)
25:    end if
26: end for

 1: ∆Echampr   ←∅; ∆Efailr ←∅

 3:   C+r (x) ←{τ ∈Cr(x) : s(τ) = 1}
 4:       if C+r (x) ̸= ∅then

 8:      Add a diagnosis-guided correction to ∆Efailr

10: end for

14: Compact entries and enforce the task capacity (Section G.1)

Algorithm 3 Skill-library update (K)
Require: Kr; completed trajectories Tr; minimum support m; success threshold θsyn
 1: Kr+1 ←a working copy of Kr
 2: for each skill k called in Tr do

E  Program Execution and Evidence Checks

E.1  Program representation and execution

The planner emits a complete JSON AST before execution. Primitive calls assign outputs to named vari-
ables, and conditional statements branch on tool outputs. Program structure is fixed before execution, while
acquired evidence determines the executed path.
    Validation checks primitive names, references, output fields, selected semantic types, statement count,
branch depth, and an explicit return_answer on every path. One model repair attempt is allowed,
alongside deterministic normalization of evidence returns. Programs are limited to 48 statements and branch
depth 4.
    Semantic primitives cover observation, navigation, visual and symbolic reasoning, task interaction, and
answer submission. Low-level camera actions are executor internals; primitive calls can consume multiple
actions. Composite skills share the episode budget with the caller. Captured physics frames are observations,
not separate planner actions. Skills return intermediate evidence; the outer program submits the answer.


E.2  Evidence checks and answer consistency

An evidence contract is a set of rules that checks the format and consistency of evidence returned by a visual
primitive. A structured result can include an answer, confidence, indices of the supporting views, visibility
statements, per-view explanations, and task-specific fields. A parsing or output-contract failure can trigger
one visual repair attempt. Valid JSON alone does not establish sufficient evidence.
   The final answer is checked against the corresponding visual output using its answer, evidence-view
references, and contract fields. Programs return the complete structured result to preserve this link.
    For tasks that check the final evidence contract, a trajectory can serve as a successful memory example
only when
                   ℓ(τ) = 1[final evidence contract passes],     s(τ) = rtask(τ)ℓ(τ).               (11)

This final gate applies to all evaluated tasks except Action Order Inference and Physical Contact. Those two
tasks retain visual-output checks and use ℓ(τ) = 1 when selecting successful memory examples.

G  Strategy and Skill Updates

G.1  Strategy records and update operations

Strategy records contain applicability conditions, lessons, recommended program changes and primitive
patterns, required actions, avoidance constraints, and supporting candidate references. They also carry
task, diagnostic, creation-round, and optional episode identifiers. Updates support addition, modification,
deletion, and retention.
    Consolidation combines three sources: the highest-advantage eligible success per episode (ties favor
fewer actions), a deterministic correction when at least two candidates agree but none achieves learning
success, and updates proposed by the frozen reflector. Compact reflection inputs include episode identifiers,
candidate answers and outcomes, action counts, advantages, all PEP scores, primary labels, and primitive
sequences; they omit images and the full simulator snapshot. Reflection processes all selected episodes in
batches of at most 20.
   Automatic capacity is max(20, 2nq) records per task, where nq is the number of selected episode bun-
dles. Compaction retains recent and supported representatives by episode, experience type, and diagnostic
dimension. During adaptation, retrieval prefers matching episodes, task specificity, recency, and support,
with a diversity pass; at most five strategies are provided. Final-round retrieval uses task-level entries.


G.2  Creating and checking skills

A composite skill stores its name, input types, program body, output, conditions for use, source trajectories,
and validation results. To create a skill, the system renames local variables and replaces eligible fixed
arguments with $input.field parameters.  It preserves variable dependencies and branches.  Fixed
strategies, directions, and relations may remain in the skill body. Answer-return statements are removed.
   The main extraction method groups programs that have the same body and input types after parameter-
ization. Each episode counts once toward a pattern’s support per update, even if several candidates contain
it. An episode counts as a success if at least one eligible occurrence succeeds. By default, a pattern needs
support from m = 2 episodes and a success ratio of at least θsyn = 0.6.
   The system also extracts broader navigation and evidence patterns from traces that pass evidence checks.
It converts up to two executed explore_scene strategies into a standard form and adds a visual primitive
for the task.  Connectivity has a separate extraction rule that produces structured connectivity evidence.

Repeated failures can also produce trial routines that check object identities and change the exploration
strategy. Thus, skill extraction does not require an exact match between program ASTs.
  New skills start in a trial state: either shadow skills or experimental corrective routines. Before trial
execution, the system checks each skill’s name, primitives, variable references, structure, and task contract.
Each skill is limited to 12 statements and branch depth 2. New skills become available in the next round.
They still require validation through actual calls, even when their source trajectories succeeded.

H  Connections to Geometric Perception and Continual Spatial Memory

Reliable embodied spatial reasoning depends on both geometric evidence acquisition and the ability to retain
useful information across observations. At the perception level, stereo matching has explored slanted-plane
cost aggregation Wang et al. [2022], disparity-classification-aware context aggregation Wang et al. [2024],
and adaptive downsampling with disparity alignment Wang et al. [2025b]. Robust geometric correspondence
has also benefited from self-supervised pseudo-labeling Wang et al. [2025d] and the adaptation of visual
foundation models Zhang et al. [2024], Wang et al. [2025c, 2026c]. At the memory level, Keep It in Mind
Wang et al. [2026b] studies continual user-centric spatial reasoning by maintaining structured memory from
streaming egocentric observations. These approaches address complementary aspects of spatial intelligence:
the stereo methods focus on recovering geometry from image pairs, whereas the memory-based approach
supports object-location reasoning over time. HiMeco targets a different challenge: rather than introducing
a stereo estimator or maintaining an explicit object-location memory, it diagnoses perception, exploration,
and planning failures from embodied execution traces and consolidates their lessons into reusable strategies
and executable skills.

                              Prompt 4: Spatial Distance Policy

  Instruction

  Spatial Distance asks which candidate has the smaller physical ground-plane distance to the
  named reference object, not which candidate appears closer to the camera.
  Perspective from one image is unreliable.  Identify the reference and both candidates, then
  collect evidence from at least two genuinely different camera positions before committing.

  Use lateral or depth parallax first; when the candidates remain at unfair image depths, move
  backward or sideways and compare again.
  A modest real elevation change followed by downward pitch is an official task strategy for
  approximating a top-down layout view.
  Call estimate_distance once with both official answer options.  Never compare two independent
  visual-assessment dictionaries or substitute apparent image size for object-to-object
  distance.
  If visibility, geometric baseline, per-view evidence, or confidence is insufficient, branch to
  a geometrically different recovery view and call estimate_distance again.

  The final answer must be one exact official option; do not expose obj_near/obj_far metadata
  names in the program or reasoning.
