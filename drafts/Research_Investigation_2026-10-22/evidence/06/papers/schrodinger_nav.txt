1  Introduction


Object navigation is a fundamental capability for mobile robots operating in human-centered envi-
ronments [1, 2, 3]. Practical service robots must find requested objects in previously unseen scenes
without pre-built maps [4, 5, 6] or costly scene-specific retraining [7, 8]. Zero-shot object navi-
gation (ZSON) formalizes this setting by requiring a robot to locate a specified object in a novel
environment without task-specific fine-tuning [9, 10].

Despite strong results in simulation, current ZSON systems often degrade in cluttered real-world
scenes with severe occlusions and latent hazards [11, 10, 12, 13]. We define a latent hazard as an
initially unobserved, unannotated region that may cause collision, unsafe traversal, or task failure if
traversed [14, 15, 16]. In these scenes, targets and risks may only become visible from revealing
viewpoints, as illustrated by the occluded cat in Figure 1. Yet many existing methods act on a single
inferred scene interpretation, making them prone to missing hidden targets or choosing unsafe paths
through uncertain regions [17, 18].

To address this limitation, we draw inspiration from Schr¨odinger’s thought experiment on uncer-
tainty and propose Schr¨odinger’s Navigator, a principled navigation framework that treats unob-
served space as a superposition of plausible future worlds and reasons over them before committing
to an action, as illustrated in Figure 1. Rather than completing the scene once, the agent reasons at
inference time over multiple trajectory-conditioned imagined 3D futures. This enables the robot to
evaluate how the world may appear along different candidate paths and plan not only from what is
observed, but also from what could be revealed behind occlusions.

At the core of our framework is a trajectory-conditioned 3D world model [19], which differs from
prior world-model navigation methods [20, 21, 22] in how imagined futures are generated and used.
Given egocentric visual observations and sampled trajectories, the model predicts hypothetical 3D
observations along each path, maintaining multiple plausible realizations of unobserved space. To
make imagination efficient and decision-relevant, we introduce an adaptive, occluder-aware trajec-
tory sampling strategy that uses local geometry and uncertainty to focus predictions on regions where
occlusion is most likely to affect navigation, avoiding costly dense rollouts over the full scene. The
imagined 3D predictions are spatially aligned and fused into a Future-Aware Value Map (FAVM).
Unlike value maps built only from observed states or short-horizon predictions, FAVM aggregates
evidence across candidate futures and assigns value to currently unobserved regions according to
their expected utility. The policy can reduce uncertainty, expose likely target locations, and avoid
hazardous regions without requiring dense global reconstruction or extra exploratory detours.

We evaluate Schr¨odinger’s Navigator in Habitat and on a physical Go2 quadruped across ObjectNav
and occlusion-heavy scenarios.  Results show that future-aware reasoning preserves competitive
ObjectNav performance while improving behavior when targets or risks are initially hidden. By
reasoning over imagined 3D futures at inference time, our method helps reveal hidden objects, avoid
uncertain blind spots, and generalize to zero-shot navigation under real-world uncertainty.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

        • We identify a fundamental limitation of existing zero-shot object navigation methods un-
        der severe occlusion and latent hazards: their reliance on single-scene inference without
          explicit reasoning over uncertainty in unobserved space.

        • We propose Schr¨odinger’s Navigator, a belief-aware navigation framework that reasons
        over multiple trajectory-conditioned imagined 3D futures. The framework combines a
         trajectory-conditioned 3D world model, adaptive occluder-aware trajectory sampling, and
        a Future-Aware Value Map (FAVM) for proactive uncertainty-aware planning.

        • We demonstrate in both Habitat simulation and real-world Go2 deployment that explicit
        reasoning over imagined 3D futures improves robustness in occlusion-heavy object search,
          earlier hidden-target discovery, and risk-aware waypoint selection.

2  Related Work



Object Navigation (ObjectNav) [23] studies embodied agents that locate target objects in unseen
environments. Task-trained approaches based on reinforcement or imitation learning [8, 1, 24, 25]
require large-scale training and often generalize poorly to cluttered real-world scenes, making sim-
to-real deployment challenging. Zero-shot approaches instead leverage pretrained vision-language
models [26, 9, 10] or large language models [27, 28, 29] for reasoning and planning without task-
specific training [12, 10, 29, 30]. These methods improve semantics-driven exploration through mul-
timodal target embeddings [9, 10], vision-language frontier maps [12], instruction-based prompting
[31, 28], and adaptive fusion of semantic and geometric cues [32, 33, 34, 35, 36]. However, zero-
shot ObjectNav remains brittle in realistic, occlusion-heavy environments and under unknown risks
[10, 12], because value estimation is still dominated by visible frontiers and lacks explicit reasoning
about hidden areas behind occluders, leading to myopic exploration.

Imagination for Navigation leverages generation models to simulate future observations and sup-
port planning [37, 20, 38, 21]. Related world-model methods learn predictive dynamics and roll
out trajectories in latent space [39, 40]. Recent navigation world models further predict egocentric
visual streams. NWM [20] predicts future trajectories and views for path ranking, while Navi-
gateDiff [38] uses diffusion-based visual prediction as a zero-shot navigation assistant. Language-
conditioned variants, such as VISTA and VISTAv2 [41, 42, 22], generate landmark images or
instruction-conditioned future views and use their alignment to guide action selection. Other works
imagine unseen scenes or predict occupancy maps to complete unobserved spaces for exploration
[43, 44, 45, 46, 47]. These works are mainly studied under visual prediction, or vision-and-language
navigation protocols. Unlike prior works, Schr¨odinger’s Navigator imagines future observations
along multiple paths with a trajectory-conditioned 3D world model and constructs a FAVM, enabling
explicit reasoning about occlusions, risks, and blind-spot targets for robust zero-shot navigation.

3  Method

We introduce Schr¨odinger’s Navigator, a zero-shot object navigation framework that reduces
occlusion-induced uncertainty by reasoning over multiple trajectory-conditioned imagined 3D fu-
tures. At each decision step, the robot receives the current navigation context, including the goal
instruction, RGB-D observation, robot pose, and accumulated map.  Instead of committing to a
single inferred scene, our method samples a compact set of occluder-aware candidate trajectories,
imagines future 3D scenes along them, and aggregates the resulting cues into a future-aware affor-
dance map for waypoint selection.

3.2  Future-Aware Navigation Decision

Future-Aware Value Map.  The imagined scene Gimgt   provides cues about free space, possible
target locations, and risky regions beyond the current observation. We convert these cues into a
Future-Aware Value Map (FAVM), denoted by mFAt   , over candidate navigation positions Pt =
{µg | g ∈Gnav}, where µg is the center of a navigable Gaussian:
          mFAt  (p) = N(αsemSt(p) + αexpEt(p) −αriskRt(p)) ,    p ∈Pt,

where St(p) measures semantic relevance to the target, Et(p) estimates the local coverage of newly
imagined free space, and Rt(p) penalizes candidate paths passing near geometrically supported
occupied regions in the imagined scenes. The weights αsem, αexp, and αrisk balance target seeking,
exploration, and safety.

Specifically, St(p) uses exponentially decayed nearest distances to observed and imagined target-
labeled Gaussians, where imagined target cues are discounted by a factor λimg < 1 to avoid over-
trusting generated content. Et(p) estimates the amount of previously unobserved but likely navi-
gable space that can be estimated around p, computed from imagined free-space primitives not yet
covered by the observed map. Rt(p) is a soft geometric risk penalty computed from filtered and
spatially deduplicated imagined occupied voxels according to their distances from the candidate
path. All three component scores are normalized to [0, 1] before fusion, and the combined FAVM
score is normalized once more. Implementation details, including kernel radius, semantic voting,
and normalization, are provided in Appendix D.

Fusion with observation-based cues. FAVM provides foresight, but navigation must remain
grounded in current observations. We combine FAVM with an observation-based multi-sourced
value map mt = mactt + msemt  + mtrajt  + mheut    , which aggregates action guidance, semantic land-
mark cues, trajectory constraints, and heuristic navigation priors.

The final affordance map is computed as

                   mafft  (p) = β ¯mt(p) + (1 −β) mFAt  (p),    p ∈Pt,

where ¯mt is the normalized observation-based value map and β ∈[0, 1] controls the balance
between grounded evidence and imagined future guidance. The navigation target is selected as

Execution.  Following an instruction-driven hierarchical planner, the goal instruction is parsed into
high-level action-landmark guidance, which contributes to the observation-based value map. The
selected navigation target p⋆t is passed to the local planner to compute executable waypoints for robot
control. To reduce latency, the world model is invoked periodically rather than at every navigation
step.

4  Experiments

We evaluate Schr¨odinger’s Navigator in both simulation and real-world settings to assess its nav-
igation performance, component effectiveness, and practical deployability. Our experiments first
compare against strong ZSON baselines on HM3D, then ablate the key design choices behind future
imagination and FAVM-based planning, and finally validate the system on a physical Go2 quadruped
in indoor environments. More qualitative results and failure cases are shown in Appendix F.


4.1  Experimental Setup

Baselines and benchmark.  We compare our approach against a broad set of representative base-
lines, including task-trained approaches (ZSON [9], PixNav [13], SPNet [48], SGM [35]), zero-shot
semantic or map-based explorers (CoW [10], ESC [28], L3MVN [49], TriHelper [50], VoroNav [51],
GAMap [33], BeliefMapNav [30]), imagination-based VLM navigators (ImagineNav [46], Imagi-
neNav++ [47]), and LLM/VLM-based planners (VLFM [12], InstructNav [31]). We conduct large-
scale simulation evaluation on HM3D [52] within Habitat [53]. Following prior work, we report
Success Rate (SR), Success weighted by Path Length (SPL), and Distance to Goal (DTG). Detailed
descriptions of baselines are provided in Appendix E.


Implementation.  We use Qwen3.6-35B-A3B [54] for instruction parsing and navigation-direction
judgment, FlashWorld [19] for trajectory-conditioned 3D imagination, and GLEE [55] for semantic
segmentation. In HM3D simulation, FlashWorld is triggered every 50 observation updates, rather
than at every step. Real-world tests use a Unitree Go2 with an RGB-D camera in Office, Classroom,
and Common Room scenes, covering static-object search and occlusion-heavy unknown-risk cases.
Hyperparameters, runtime, compute, and deployment details are provided in Appendices A and G.


4.2  Simulation Results

4.2.1  Quantitative Results

Table 1 presents the quantitative results on HM3D. Our method achieves the highest SR of 0.682,
outperforming the strongest baseline, ImagineNav++ (0.625), by 5.7 percentage points.  It also at-
tains the lowest reported DTG of 1.84 m, compared with 2.89 m for InstructNav. Meanwhile, our

results demonstrate the effectiveness of future-aware navigation in improving target discovery and
goal proximity while maintaining competitive path efficiency. Further analysis across different oc-
clusion severity levels is provided in Appendix E (Table 6).

4.3  Real-World Deployment

Our real-world experiments provide a controlled evaluation against an InstructNav-style baseline,
complementing the broader comparisons with navigation methods conducted on HM3D.

We evaluate adaptability under two task settings: (1) Static Object Search, where the agent explores
standard scenes to locate objects; and (2) Unknown-Risk Scenarios, where severe static occlusions
are not visible from afar, testing the agent’s ability to anticipate uncertainty and avoid blind spots.


4.3.1  Performance Analysis

As shown in Table 3, we report success counts over 30 trials per setting, with 10 trials in each of the
three environments.

In standard static object search, Schr¨odinger’s Navigator performs comparably to InstructNav,
achieving a slightly higher success rate (23/30 vs.  22/30), indicating that future-aware reason-
ing does not compromise regular object-search performance. In scenarios with unknown risks, our
method shows a clearer advantage, succeeding in 17/30 trials compared with InstructNav’s 11/30.
This suggests that imagined 3D futures help the robot better handle occlusions, ambiguous observa-
tions, and potential blind spots.

Overall, these real-world results show that our method maintains strong static-search performance
while improving robustness in risk-aware navigation. Qualitative examples are provided in Figure 5.

5  Conclusion

Schr¨odinger’s Navigator addresses ZSON limitations in occluded environments by leveraging a 3D
trajectory-conditioned world model to imagine and fuse plausible 3D futures into a unified scene.
This training-free framework constructs a single affordance map encoding semantics, exploration,
and risk. Real-world experiments on a Unitree Go2 demonstrate its robustness, achieving compara-
ble performance in static tasks while outperforming the baseline in occlusion-heavy scenarios.

6  Limitations

Schr¨odinger’s Navigator uses imagined 3D futures as plausible hypotheses rather than exact re-
constructions of hidden space. While FAVM discounts generated cues and grounds decisions in
current observations, inaccurate generation, semantic transfer errors, or imperfect depth alignment
may still bias waypoint selection when imagined evidence is over-weighted. Our method performs
risk-aware waypoint selection but does not provide formal safety guarantees. Future work could im-
prove reliability through uncertainty-calibrated imagination, stronger multi-view consistency, and
active verification of uncertain regions. In addition, the current tri-trajectory sampler focuses on
the dominant forward occluder for efficiency, but more complex layouts with multiple occluders or
non-frontal blind spots may require adaptive trajectory budgeting. Broader evaluation across more
diverse environments, dynamic obstacles, sensors, and robot platforms, as well as integration with
certified local planners, would further strengthen real-world deployment.

A.2  FAVM, perception, and runtime settings

We use Qwen3.6-35B-A3B [54] for instruction parsing and direction judgment, FlashWorld [19] for
trajectory-conditioned 3D imagination, and GLEE [55] for open-vocabulary semantic segmentation.

exported at 15 fps.

A.3  Runtime characteristics

Trig./Ep. denotes FlashWorld invocations per episode; Lat./Trig. denotes per-trigger world-model
service time; Wait Ratio is the fraction of episode time waiting for fresh future evidence; and
Waypoint Change measures the fraction of navigation decisions in which the selected waypoint
differs from that selected using the observation-only map.  In HM3D simulation, FlashWorld is
triggered at a fixed interval of 50 observation updates. In real-world execution it runs asynchronously
while the local planner continues executing the current waypoint. In our experiments, N = 1/3/5
futures take about 3.0/9.0/15.0s per trigger. The full model triggers 4.24 times per episode, with a
20.1% waiting ratio and a 46.8% waypoint-change rate.

E  Baselines, Evaluation Protocol, and Metrics

E.1  Simulation benchmark and metrics

We evaluate on HM3D within Habitat. Following the ObjectNav protocol, each episode is capped at
a maximum of 500 steps and is considered successful if the agent stops within 1.0 m of an in-
stance of the target category. We report Success Rate (SR), Success weighted by Path Length
(SPL), and Distance to Goal (DTG). SR measures the fraction of successful episodes. SPL re-
wards successful episodes with shorter paths. DTG measures the final geodesic distance from the
agent to the target object. In HM3D simulation, the selected waypoints are executed using Habitat’s
ShortestPathFollower with discrete navigation actions.

We compare against four groups of baselines.


Task-trained approaches.  ZSON [9], PixNav [13], SPNet [48], and SGM [35] rely on task-
specific training or learned navigation policies. These methods can perform well in their training
domains but are less aligned with the training-free zero-shot setting targeted by our method.


Zero-shot geometric and semantic explorers.  ESC [28], L3MVN [49], TriHelper [50], VoroNav
[51], GAMap [33], and BeliefMapNav [30] use geometric frontiers, semantic maps, language pri-
ors, or belief maps to guide exploration without task-specific fine-tuning. They improve semantic

E.2  Real-world protocol


Real-world experiments are conducted on a Unitree Go2 quadruped equipped with an RGB-D cam-
era in three indoor environments: Office, Classroom, and Common Room. We evaluate two settings.
In Static Object Search, the target is a static object in a standard indoor scene. In Unknown-Risk
Scenarios, the scene includes severe occlusion or blind spots that may hide targets or risky regions
from the initial viewpoint. Each scene contains 10 trials per setting, and success is reported as
Success / Total Trials.
